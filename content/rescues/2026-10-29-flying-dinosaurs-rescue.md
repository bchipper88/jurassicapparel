## Replacement copy (body only, starts below the theme's H1)

**The giant "flying dinosaurs" in Jurassic Park weren't dinosaurs at all.** Pteranodon, Quetzalcoatlus and the
classic "pterodactyl" were pterosaurs, flying reptiles that were close cousins of the dinosaurs and evolved flight
on their own. But some real dinosaurs *did* fly. They were small, feathered, and they're the reason there are birds
outside your window.

This guide gives you the flying dinosaur names people actually search for, sorted into the two groups. It covers how
big each one got, when and where it lived, and how it got into the air.

> **The short answer**
> - **Flying reptiles (not dinosaurs):** Pteranodon, Quetzalcoatlus, Pterodactylus, Rhamphorhynchus, Dimorphodon,
>   Hatzegopteryx. These are the "pterodactyls" of films and toy boxes.
> - **Dinosaurs that really flew or glided:** Archaeopteryx, Microraptor, Anchiornis, Changyuraptor, Rahonavis, and
>   the bat-winged Yi qi.
> - **Dinosaurs that still fly:** every bird. A sparrow is a living theropod dinosaur.

## Were pterosaurs dinosaurs?

**No. Pterosaurs were flying reptiles, not dinosaurs.** This is the most common mix-up in paleontology, and it's
an easy one to make: pterosaurs lived alongside dinosaurs for their whole run, and films have called them
dinosaurs for a century.

| Feature | Dinosaurs | Pterosaurs |
|---|---|---|
| **Group** | Dinosauria | Pterosauria |
| **Hips and legs** | Hip socket open, legs held straight under the body | Different hip and ankle structure |
| **First known** | about 230 million years ago | Late Triassic, more than 215 million years ago |
| **Relationship** | Cousins: both are archosaurs, the group that also includes crocodiles | |

Pterosaurs were the **first vertebrates to fly under their own power**. They beat birds into the air by more than
50 million years, and bats by well over 100 million.

Think of it like family: pterosaurs are the dinosaurs' cousins, not their siblings. Related, but a different
household.

### Why everyone says "pterodactyl"

*Pterodactylus* was the first pterosaur ever described, from a fossil found in Bavaria in the 1780s. The name
stuck, and "pterodactyl" became the everyday word for every flying reptile. Scientists don't use it as a group
name. It's one genus, about the size of a crow.

## The pterosaurs: famous flying reptile names

These animals ruled the skies for about 150 million years, far longer than birds have so far.

**Quetzalcoatlus.** The giant. Its wingspan was 10–11 m (33–36 ft), the size of a small plane, and on the ground its
head reached about the height of a giraffe's. It still weighed only around 200–250 kg (440–550 lb), because its
bones were hollow, air-filled and thin-walled. It's named after Quetzalcoatl, the Aztec feathered-serpent god, and
it may have stalked small animals on foot like a giant stork when it wasn't flying.

**Hatzegopteryx.** Quetzalcoatlus's heavier-built cousin from Romania, which was an island at the time. With a
wingspan of about 10 m and a skull built for force, it may have been the top predator on that island, where the
dinosaurs were small.

**Pteranodon.** The one most people picture. Up to about 7 m (23 ft) across, with a long backward-pointing crest and
no teeth at all: the name means "toothless wing". It fished over the inland sea that once covered Kansas, and more
than a thousand specimens have been found, which makes it one of the best-known pterosaurs.

**Pterodactylus.** The original "pterodactyl", about crow-sized, with a short tail and a long toothy beak. It lived
around a tropical lagoon in what is now southern Germany.

**Rhamphorhynchus.** From the same German lagoon. It had a long, needle-toothed snout for catching fish and a long
tail ending in a diamond-shaped vane. Some fossils still hold fish in the stomach.

**Dimorphodon.** An early Jurassic pterosaur from the Dorset coast of England, found by Mary Anning in 1828. It had a big, deep head, and its name means "two-form tooth": fangs at the front, smaller teeth behind,
which is unusual for a reptile.

**Eudimorphodon.** One of the oldest pterosaurs known, from about 210 million years ago in northern Italy. It shows
that pterosaurs were already fully formed fliers in the Triassic.

**Tupandactylus and Tapejara.** Brazilian pterosaurs with enormous sail-shaped head crests, in Tupandactylus's
case far taller than the skull itself. One fossil kept the soft tissue, showing the crest was even bigger in life
than the bone suggests. It was almost certainly for display.

**Pterodaustro.** An Argentine filter-feeder with an upturned jaw full of around a thousand bristle-like teeth. It
strained tiny animals from shallow water, much as a flamingo does today.

**Nyctosaurus.** A Kansas soarer with a huge, antler-like crest and no clawed fingers on its hands at all, only the
wing finger. That would have made it a clumsy walker but a very efficient glider over the sea.

**Dearc.** Announced in 2022 from the Isle of Skye, Scotland. With a wingspan over 2.5 m, it's the largest
pterosaur known from the Jurassic, so pterosaurs were getting big long before Quetzalcoatlus.

### How did pterosaurs fly?

A pterosaur wing was a sheet of skin and muscle stretched from a hugely lengthened **fourth finger** back to the
leg. That one finger could be longer than the rest of the arm. Stiffening fibres ran through the membrane to give
it shape, and the three other fingers stayed small and clawed for climbing and walking.

On the ground most pterosaurs walked on all fours, with the folded wings working as front legs. To take off, the
current best explanation is a **"quad launch"**: a leaping push-up that used the powerful arms to vault the animal
into the air. That's how even Quetzalcoatlus could get off flat ground without a run-up. Once airborne, the big ones
soared on rising air, much as albatrosses and condors do.

Many pterosaurs were covered in fine, hair-like filaments called **pycnofibres**. They weren't the bald, leathery
animals of old illustrations.

## Dinosaurs that actually flew

**Yes, some real dinosaurs flew, and they looked nothing like pterosaurs.** While pterosaurs held the skies, small
feathered theropods, the same branch as Velociraptor and T. rex, were evolving feathered wings of their own. One
line of them became birds.

**Archaeopteryx.** Found in Germany in 1861, two years after Darwin's *On the Origin of Species*, and long called
"the first bird". About raven-sized, it had teeth, clawed fingers and a long bony tail, along with fully formed
flight feathers. Today it's best described as a flying dinosaur sitting very close to the start of the bird line.
*Read more: [Archaeopteryx, the bridge between dinosaurs and birds](https://jurassicapparel.com/blogs/dinosaur-facts/archaeopteryx-the-bridge-between-dinosaurs-and-birds).*

**Anchiornis.** A pigeon-sized feathered dinosaur from about 160 million years ago, with long feathers on its
legs as well as its arms. Its colours are known from preserved pigment structures: grey-black body, white-banded
wings and a reddish crown.

**Microraptor.** The famous four-winged dinosaur. A crow-sized dromaeosaur (the raptor family) from China, with
flight feathers on both arms and legs, and an iridescent black sheen like a grackle's. It could certainly glide
between trees, and some studies argue it could flap well enough to manage short powered flight. Fossils show it
ate fish, birds and small mammals.
*Read more: [Microraptor](https://jurassicapparel.com/blogs/dinosaur-facts/microraptor).*

**Changyuraptor.** A larger four-winged raptor, roughly turkey-sized at about 4 kg, with tail feathers around
30 cm long, the longest known on any non-bird dinosaur. That tail probably helped it slow down and land.

**Rahonavis.** A raven-sized flier from Madagascar with a raptor's sickle claw on each foot. Scientists still
argue whether it's an early bird or a flying raptor. Either way, it flew.

**Confuciusornis.** An early bird from China, around 125 million years ago, and the first known with a toothless,
horny beak. Hundreds of fossils have been found, some in groups, so it was common.

**Ichthyornis.** A toothed seabird about the size of a tern, which fished the same Kansas sea as Pteranodon. A bird
with a beak full of teeth, living among dinosaurs.

### Gliders with bat wings: Yi qi and Ambopteryx

The strangest flying dinosaurs grew skin membranes instead of big feathers. **Yi qi** ("strange wing"), described in
2015, and **Ambopteryx** were tiny Chinese scansoriopterygids with a long rod of bone at the wrist holding up a
membrane wing. They were gliders, not powered fliers, and the experiment died out. Feathers won.

## Could a raptor fly?

**Velociraptor couldn't fly.** It was about the size of a turkey, with arms far too short for wings. But it did have
feathers: a 2007 study found quill knobs on a Velociraptor forearm, the bumps where wing feathers anchor in birds.
So it probably had feathered "wings" it couldn't fly with, the way an ostrich does.

The raptors that *could* get airborne were its small cousins: **Microraptor**, **Changyuraptor** and, probably,
**Rahonavis**. If you're searching for a "flying raptor", those are the three.

## Could T. rex fly?

**No.** An adult Tyrannosaurus rex weighed somewhere around 8 tonnes, and its arms were only about a metre long. Nothing
about it could leave the ground. Young T. rex may have had a coat of fuzzy proto-feathers, which is probably where
the "flying T. rex" idea comes from, but those were for warmth, not flight.

T. rex is still related to the birds, though. Both are theropods, so a chicken is a closer relative of T. rex than
T. rex is of Triceratops.

## Birds are dinosaurs (and they fly)

The most successful flying dinosaurs are still around. Birds aren't just "descended from" dinosaurs in a loose
sense. They *are* dinosaurs, classified inside the theropods alongside T. rex and Velociraptor.

When the asteroid hit 66 million years ago, every non-bird dinosaur and every pterosaur died out. A few small,
feathered, ground-dwelling birds survived, and they've since become the 10,000-plus bird species alive today. So
the honest answer to "are there any flying dinosaurs alive?" is yes: about 10,000 kinds, including the pigeon on
your windowsill.

## Flying dinosaurs vs. pterosaurs: the key differences

| Feature | Flying dinosaurs (birds and kin) | Pterosaurs |
|---|---|---|
| **Wing** | Feathers along the arm (and sometimes the legs) | Skin membrane on one long fourth finger |
| **Body covering** | Feathers | Pycnofibres (fine filaments) |
| **First appeared** | about 165–160 million years ago | more than 215 million years ago |
| **Biggest** | Pelagornis, 6–7.4 m (after the dinosaurs) | Quetzalcoatlus, 10–11 m |
| **Descendants** | Every living bird | None: all died out 66 million years ago |

Powered flight evolved **three times** in vertebrates: pterosaurs, birds and bats. Each built its wing on a
different plan. Pterosaurs used one long finger, bats spread all of theirs, and birds use feathers on the whole arm.
Gliding evolved many more times, including in the bat-winged dinosaurs above. It's a textbook case of convergent
evolution, where unrelated animals arrive at the same answer because the problem, getting into the air, is the same.

## Other prehistoric flying creatures

Not everything that flew in prehistory was a pterosaur or a bird.

- **Meganeura** — a griffinfly, a giant relative of dragonflies, with a wingspan of about 70 cm. It flew about
  300 million years ago, before the dinosaurs.
- **Coelurosauravus** — a small Permian reptile, about 255 million years ago, that glided on skin stretched over
  rod-like bones along its sides. It's the earliest known gliding vertebrate.
- **Sharovipteryx** — a Triassic reptile from Central Asia that glided on membranes between its *hind* legs.
- **Argentavis** — a giant bird from Argentina, about 6 million years ago, with a wingspan estimated at 5–7 m.
  It's one of the largest flying birds ever.
- **Pelagornis sandersi** — a seabird with bony "teeth" on its beak and a wingspan of 6–7.4 m, about 25 million
  years ago. It's the largest flying bird known.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the biggest flying dinosaur ever?

If you mean pterosaurs, it's **Quetzalcoatlus**, at 10–11 m across, with Hatzegopteryx close behind. If you mean
real dinosaurs, the biggest flier is a bird that lived after the asteroid: **Pelagornis**, at up to 7.4 m. Among
the feathered dinosaurs of the Mesozoic, the four-winged **Changyuraptor** was one of the largest that could get
airborne.

### What was the first flying dinosaur?

The oldest feathered dinosaurs with wing-like limbs, such as **Anchiornis**, date to about 160 million years ago.
**Archaeopteryx**, at about 150 million years, is the oldest widely accepted flier on the bird line. Pterosaurs were
flying more than 60 million years earlier, but they aren't dinosaurs.

### What is a small flying dinosaur called?

Most real flying dinosaurs were small. **Microraptor** was crow-sized, **Anchiornis** pigeon-sized, and **Yi qi**
smaller still. Among pterosaurs, **Pterodactylus** was about the size of a crow.

### Could a pterodactyl pick up a person?

No. Even Quetzalcoatlus, the largest, weighed around 200–250 kg, and flying animals can only lift a fraction of their own
weight. The person-snatching pterosaurs in films are fiction.

### What dinosaur is closest to a dragon?

The big azhdarchid pterosaurs, Quetzalcoatlus and Hatzegopteryx, come closest: long necks, huge wings, a predator's
build. Among actual dinosaurs, **Dracorex** ("dragon king") has the name and a spiky, horned skull, but it was a
pachycephalosaur, a bone-headed plant-eater that walked on two legs and never flew.

### Did T. rex have feathers?

Probably some, at least when young. Its close relatives had feathery coats, and the small *Yutyrannus*, a
tyrannosaur relative, is known with long filaments. Skin impressions from adult T. rex show scales, so the grown
animal was probably mostly scaly. Either way, it didn't fly.

### Are there any flying dinosaurs alive today?

Yes. Every bird is a dinosaur, so every bird that flies, from hummingbirds to albatrosses, is a living flying
dinosaur.
