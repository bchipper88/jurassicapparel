# The Hell Creek Formation: A Field Guide to the Last Days of the Dinosaurs

You've probably heard the name on a museum label or in a documentary, usually right next to a T. rex. The **Hell
Creek Formation** is a band of sandstone and mudstone in eastern Montana and the western Dakotas, laid down by rivers
between about 67 and 66 million years ago. It holds the last dinosaurs that lived in North America before the asteroid,
and in some places you can put your finger on the thin line of clay where they stop.

This guide covers what Hell Creek actually is, what lived there, the famous skeletons that came out of it, the strange
Tanis site, and how you can go see it, or even dig in it, yourself.

## The Hell Creek Formation at a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Where** | Eastern Montana, western North Dakota, northwestern South Dakota, a corner of northeastern Wyoming |
| **Age** | Late Cretaceous (Maastrichtian), roughly the last 1–2 million years before 66 million years ago |
| **Rock** | Gray, tan and purple mudstones and siltstones, channel sandstones, thin coal (lignite) beds |
| **Thickness** | Up to a few hundred feet, thickest in eastern Montana |
| **How it formed** | Rivers and swamps on a coastal plain, draining east into a shrinking inland sea |
| **Below it** | Fox Hills Sandstone: beach and nearshore sands of that sea |
| **Above it** | Fort Union Formation (Tullock Member in Montana, Ludlow Member in North Dakota): the first rocks after the dinosaurs |
| **Named** | By Barnum Brown, 1907, after Hell Creek near Jordan, Montana |
| **Best-known fossils** | *Tyrannosaurus rex*, *Triceratops*, *Edmontosaurus*, the K-Pg boundary clay |

It's a "formation" in the geologist's sense: a body of rock distinctive enough to map across the landscape and give a
name. Rocks of the same age turn up under other names nearby: the **Lance Formation** in Wyoming, the **Frenchman
Formation** in Saskatchewan and the **Scollard Formation** in Alberta. They're all slices of the same ancient
landscape.

## Where is Hell Creek, and why is it called that?

The type area, the place geologists treat as the reference, is the badlands along Hell Creek in Garfield County,
Montana, north of the little town of Jordan, where the creek runs toward what is now Fort Peck Lake. It's rough,
dry, crumbling country, which is exactly why fossils show up there: nothing much grows on the steep gray slopes, and
every rainstorm washes away a little more rock and exposes new bone.

Barnum Brown, the American Museum of Natural History's star fossil hunter, worked these badlands from 1902. That first
summer he found the skeleton that Henry Fairfield Osborn named *Tyrannosaurus rex* in 1905. Brown described the rocks
themselves as the "Hell Creek beds" in 1907, and the name stuck. In 1966 the National Park Service recognized a
stretch of the area as the **Hell Creek Fossil Area National Natural Landmark**.

From Garfield County the formation spreads east and south, across eastern Montana (Makoshika State Park outside
Glendive is a great place to see it), into southwestern North Dakota around Marmarth and Bowman, and into northwestern
South Dakota around Faith and the Grand River country.

## Reading the rocks: how Hell Creek formed

Stand at the bottom of a Hell Creek badland and walk up, and you're walking forward through time.

**At the base** sits the Fox Hills Sandstone, the beaches and shallow seafloor of the Western Interior Seaway, the
inland sea that once split North America in two. As that sea drained away, rivers flowing east off the young Rocky
Mountains spread mud and sand over the old shoreline. That river-built layer is Hell Creek.

**In the middle** you see the rivers themselves. Thick, cross-bedded sandstones are old channels, the sandbars where
carcasses washed up and got buried. That's where most big skeletons are found. The banded gray and purple mudstones
between them are old floodplains, ponds and soils. Thin dark seams of lignite are the remains of swampy forests.

**At the top**, in the right places, is the boundary. Right around the contact with the overlying Fort Union rocks,
often just under a coal seam that Montana geologists call the "Z coal", sits a thin clay layer, sometimes only a
centimeter or so thick. That's the **K-Pg boundary**: debris that fell out of the sky after the asteroid struck the
Yucatán 66 million years ago. It's rich in iridium, an element rare in Earth's crust but common in asteroids, and it
holds tiny glass beads and shocked quartz grains that only an impact can make.

Below that line there are non-bird dinosaurs. Above it, there are none. High-precision dating of ash beds in the Hell
Creek region (Sprain and colleagues, 2015) puts the boundary at about 66.0 million years ago, which makes Hell Creek
one of the best places on Earth to study what happened right before and right after.

## What lived in the Hell Creek world

Hell Creek has been collected for more than a century, so its list of residents is long. Picture a warm, humid river
plain, closer to today's Gulf Coast than to modern Montana, with forests along the water and no grass anywhere yet.

### The dinosaurs

| Dinosaur | What it was | How common |
|---|---|---|
| ***Triceratops*** | Three-horned plant eater, up to about 30 feet long | The most common big dinosaur in the formation |
| ***Edmontosaurus*** | Duck-billed plant eater with hundreds of grinding teeth | Common, often in bonebeds |
| ***Tyrannosaurus rex*** | The top predator | Surprisingly common (see the census below) |
| ***Torosaurus*** | Big-frilled horned dinosaur; some argue it's an old *Triceratops* | Rare |
| ***Thescelosaurus*** | Turkey-to-pony-sized plant eater | Fairly common |
| ***Pachycephalosaurus*** | Dome-headed plant eater | Known mostly from skull domes |
| ***Ankylosaurus*** and *Denversaurus* | Armored plant eaters | Rare |
| ***Struthiomimus*** / *Ornithomimus* | Ostrich-like, fast runners | Uncommon |
| ***Anzu wyliei*** | A 500-pound, beaked, feathered "chicken from hell" (named 2014) | Rare |
| ***Acheroraptor*** | A raptor, *Velociraptor*'s North American cousin (named 2013) | Known from jaws and teeth |

Between 1999 and 2010, Jack Horner and the Museum of the Rockies ran the **Hell Creek Project**, one of the biggest
organized surveys any fossil formation has had. Their 2011 census counted the dinosaur specimens they found: about 40
percent were *Triceratops*, and *Tyrannosaurus* and *Edmontosaurus* each made up close to a quarter. A top predator as
common as its main prey surprised a lot of people. One explanation is that T. rex bones are big and tough and survive
better; another is that we've been underestimating how many T. rex there were.

There's also a live argument about one more name. The small tyrannosaur skulls from Hell Creek and the Lance
Formation have been called *Nanotyrannus*, "dwarf tyrant", or else written off as teenage T. rex. In 2025, Lindsay
Zanno and James Napoli studied the tyrannosaur in North Carolina's "Dueling Dinosaurs" specimen and argued in *Nature*
that it's a nearly grown adult of a separate species, so *Nanotyrannus* is real. Not everyone agrees yet. Watch this
one.

If you want the full picture of the big predator's range beyond Hell Creek, our map of
[where T. rex lived](/blogs/blog/where-did-t-rex-live) covers every state and province it's been found in.

### Everyone else

The dinosaurs get the headlines, but most Hell Creek fossils are small:

- **Crocodilians** like *Borealosuchus* and the blunt-snouted *Brachychampsa*.
- **Champsosaurs**, long-snouted reptiles that looked like gharials and hunted fish in the rivers.
- **Turtles**, lots of them, including softshells and big snapping-turtle relatives.
- **Fish**: gars, sturgeon, paddlefish and bowfin, many of them families still alive today.
- **Mammals**, most no bigger than a rat. The biggest was *Didelphodon*, an otter-sized, shell-crushing relative of
  marsupials. Multituberculates, rodent-like mammals now long gone, were everywhere.
- **Frogs, salamanders, lizards and snakes**, plus rare birds and pterosaurs.

### The plants

Fossil leaves show a forest of flowering trees and shrubs, relatives of sycamores, laurels and magnolias, mixed with
conifers, ferns, cycad-like plants and ginkgos. Kirk Johnson's work on the leaf fossils of North Dakota found that well
over half of the plant species below the boundary don't show up above it. The asteroid didn't just end the dinosaurs.
It reset the forest.

## Famous fossils that came out of Hell Creek

A good share of the world's most famous dinosaurs came out of these rocks:

- **The first T. rex** (CM 9380). Barnum Brown, 1902, Garfield County, Montana. Now at the Carnegie Museum of Natural
  History in Pittsburgh.
- **"Sue"** (FMNH PR 2081). Found by Sue Hendrickson in 1990 near Faith, South Dakota. About 90 percent complete by
  bulk, still one of the most complete T. rex skeletons known. Sold at auction in 1997 for $8.36 million to the Field
  Museum in Chicago, where it's on display.
- **"Stan"** (BHI 3033). Found in 1987 in South Dakota and excavated by the Black Hills Institute. It sold for $31.8
  million in 2020, a record at the time, and went to the Natural History Museum in Abu Dhabi.
- **The "Wankel rex"** (MOR 555). Found by Kathy Wankel in 1988 near Fort Peck Lake, Montana. Now the "Nation's T.
  rex" at the Smithsonian in Washington, D.C.
- **"B-rex"** (MOR 1125). The Montana T. rex in which Mary Schweitzer reported soft, stretchy tissue inside a thigh
  bone in 2005, which started a whole field of research into ancient proteins.
- **"Jane"**. A young tyrannosaur from Carter County, Montana, at the Burpee Museum in Rockford, Illinois, and one of
  the specimens at the center of the *Nanotyrannus* debate.
- **The "Dueling Dinosaurs"**. A *Triceratops* and a small tyrannosaur found together in Montana in 2006, now on
  display, still in their rock, at the North Carolina Museum of Natural Sciences in Raleigh.
- **"Dakota"**. An *Edmontosaurus* "mummy" with fossilized skin, found in 1999 by Tyler Lyson near Marmarth, North
  Dakota. It's at the North Dakota Heritage Center in Bismarck.
- **"Big John"**. One of the largest *Triceratops* skeletons known, dug up in South Dakota in 2014.

That's a lot of the dinosaur gallery at your nearest big museum, all from a few counties of badland. For more on the
formation's most common big dinosaur, see our [Triceratops facts guide](/blogs/blog/triceratops-facts-everything-you-need-to-know-about-the-three-horned-giant).

## Tanis: the site that may record the day the asteroid hit

The most dramatic claim to come out of Hell Creek is **Tanis**, a site in southwestern North Dakota excavated by
paleontologist Robert DePalma. In 2019 DePalma and colleagues described it in *PNAS* as a jumbled layer of river
sediment, fish, plants and bits of other animals, all dumped together by a surge of water, and peppered with the tiny
glass beads thrown out by the impact.

Some of the fish have those glass beads in their gills. The team's reading is that the beads were raining down while
the fish were still alive, minutes to hours after the asteroid hit Mexico some 2,000 miles away, and that ground
shaking from the impact sloshed the river out of its banks and buried everything at once. Later studies of the fish
bones, including one led by Melanie During in *Nature* in 2022, suggest the impact came in the Northern Hemisphere's
spring.

Tanis is exciting and still debated. Some scientists have questioned parts of the work, and much of the material is
still being studied. So treat it as a remarkable site with a striking interpretation, not a closed case. Either way,
it sits right at the top of the Hell Creek Formation, exactly where the boundary says it should.

## The K-Pg boundary: the line where the dinosaurs stop

That thin clay layer at the top of Hell Creek is why geologists keep coming back. In a single exposure you can see:

1. **Below the line:** dinosaur bones, Cretaceous pollen and leaves, a diverse forest.
2. **The line itself:** iridium-rich clay, impact spherules, shocked quartz.
3. **Just above it:** a "fern spike", a layer where fern spores suddenly dominate, as ferns recolonized burned and
   flattened ground. Then a much poorer forest.
4. **Higher up:** mammals start to get bigger and more varied. Some of the earliest primate relatives, such as
   *Purgatorius*, come from the Tullock rocks just above Hell Creek in Montana.

Because the rivers kept laying down sediment straight through the event, Hell Creek is one of the few places where
you can follow the same landscape from the last dinosaurs into the world that replaced them.

## Can you visit Hell Creek, or dig there?

Yes, and this is the part most guides leave out. Hell Creek is one of the few dinosaur formations in the world where
ordinary families can join a dig.

**Places to see the rocks and the fossils:**

- **Makoshika State Park**, Glendive, Montana. Montana's largest state park, with Hell Creek badlands, trails and a
  visitor center with a *Triceratops* skull.
- **Garfield County Museum**, Jordan, Montana. Small museum in the heart of the type area.
- **Fort Peck Interpretive Center**, Fort Peck, Montana. Displays on the region's fossils, including a T. rex cast.
- **Carter County Museum**, Ekalaka, Montana. Montana's oldest county museum, with a strong Hell Creek collection.
- **Museum of the Rockies**, Bozeman, Montana. Home of the Hell Creek Project's finds and one of the biggest dinosaur
  collections in the country.
- **Pioneer Trails Regional Museum**, Bowman, North Dakota, and the **North Dakota Heritage Center**, Bismarck (Dakota
  the mummy).
- Farther afield: the **Field Museum** (Sue), the **Smithsonian** (the Nation's T. rex), the **Carnegie Museum** (the
  first T. rex), the **North Carolina Museum of Natural Sciences** (the Dueling Dinosaurs).

**Digs you can join:** several museums and research groups in Montana and the Dakotas run paid public field programs
each summer, typically June through August, where you work alongside paleontologists on real Hell Creek quarries.
Programs and prices change every year, so check directly with the museums above (Carter County Museum and Pioneer
Trails Regional Museum are good places to start) and with groups like the Marmarth Research Foundation in North
Dakota.

**The rules, which matter:**

- **On federal land** (Bureau of Land Management, national grasslands, the Charles M. Russell National Wildlife
  Refuge around Fort Peck), you cannot collect vertebrate fossils without a research permit under the 2009
  Paleontological Resources Preservation Act. Look, photograph, leave it.
- **On state land,** permits are required too.
- **On private land,** fossils belong to the landowner. Montana's Supreme Court confirmed in 2020 that dinosaur
  fossils aren't "minerals" under state law, so they go with the surface, not the mineral rights. Always ask
  permission first.
- If you find something big, don't dig it out. Note where it is and call a museum. Bones pulled out without a record
  of where they lay lose most of their scientific value.

## Hell Creek facts for kids

- Hell Creek is named after a real creek in Montana's badlands.
- The very first *Tyrannosaurus rex* ever found came out of Hell Creek, in 1902.
- *Triceratops* is the most common big dinosaur found there.
- Hell Creek had no grass. Grass hadn't spread yet.
- The rocks were made by rivers, and most big skeletons are found in old river sand.
- Crocodiles, turtles and gar fish lived in the same rivers as T. rex.
- At the very top of Hell Creek is a thin layer of dust left by the asteroid.
- Some fish at the Tanis site have asteroid glass beads stuck in their gills.
- Montana, North Dakota and South Dakota all have Hell Creek rocks.
- You can sign up to dig for real dinosaur bones there in summer.

## FAQ

**What is the Hell Creek Formation?**
A layer of Late Cretaceous rock, mostly mudstone and sandstone laid down by rivers, found in eastern Montana and the
western Dakotas. It's one of the richest dinosaur fossil beds in the world and records the last 1–2 million years
before the asteroid impact.

**How old is the Hell Creek Formation?**
Roughly 67 to 66 million years old. The top of the formation, at the K-Pg boundary, has been dated to about 66.0
million years ago.

**Why is it called Hell Creek?**
Barnum Brown named the rocks in 1907 after Hell Creek, a stream in the badlands of Garfield County, Montana, near the
town of Jordan, where he'd been digging since 1902.

**What dinosaurs are found in the Hell Creek Formation?**
*Triceratops*, *Edmontosaurus* and *Tyrannosaurus rex* are the most common. Others include *Torosaurus*,
*Thescelosaurus*, *Pachycephalosaurus*, *Ankylosaurus*, ornithomimids, *Anzu* and the raptor *Acheroraptor*.

**Was Sue the T. rex found in Hell Creek?**
Yes. Sue was found in 1990 in Hell Creek rocks near Faith, South Dakota, and is now at the Field Museum in Chicago.

**Can you see the asteroid layer at Hell Creek?**
In some places, yes. It's a thin clay layer at or near the top of the formation, usually right around the contact with
the Fort Union rocks above. Ask at a local museum or visitor center where it's exposed and accessible.

**Can I keep a fossil I find at Hell Creek?**
Only with the landowner's permission on private land. On federal and state land, vertebrate fossils (dinosaurs,
turtles, crocodiles, mammals) can't be collected without a permit.

**What is the Tanis site?**
A fossil site at the top of the Hell Creek Formation in North Dakota where a surge of water buried fish, plants and
other animals together with debris from the impact. Its excavators argue it records the first hours after the asteroid
hit. It's still under study and debate.
