# Where Did T. Rex Live? A Map of Every Place Tyrannosaurus Rex Has Been Found

Maybe your kid just asked whether a T. rex ever walked through your town, or you're planning a museum trip and want to
know where the real bones came from. So: **T. rex, where did it live?** In western North America, between about 68 and
66 million years ago. Its bones have been dug up from Alberta and Saskatchewan in Canada down to Texas and New Mexico,
with the richest beds in Montana and the Dakotas. It lived nowhere else on Earth that we know of.

That answer comes with a twist: the North America T. rex knew was a different shape from the one on today's maps. Below
is the state-by-state list, what the land looked like, who T. rex shared it with, and how its ancestors got there.

## The short answer

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Which continent? | North America, and only North America |
| Which part? | The west: the Great Plains and Rocky Mountain states, and Canada's prairie provinces |
| When? | About 68 to 66 million years ago, at the very end of the Cretaceous period |
| What kind of place? | Warm, wet river plains and forests near the shore of a shallow inland sea |
| Best fossil site? | The Hell Creek Formation of Montana and the Dakotas |
| Did it live in Asia? | No. Its close cousin *Tarbosaurus* did, and T. rex's ancestors probably came from there |

## Where did T. rex live? A state-by-state map

Every T. rex fossil ever found comes from rock laid down at the end of the Cretaceous in western North America. Here's
where those rocks come to the surface and what each place has given us.

| Where | Rock layer | What's been found |
|---|---|---|
| **Montana** | Hell Creek Formation | The very first T. rex (Barnum Brown, 1902, near Jordan). The "Wankel rex" now at the Smithsonian, "Wyrex", "Trix" and dozens more. More T. rex has come out of Montana than anywhere else. |
| **South Dakota** | Hell Creek Formation | "Sue" (found 1990, now at Chicago's Field Museum) and "Stan" (found 1987), two of the most complete skeletons ever dug up |
| **North Dakota** | Hell Creek Formation | Many teeth and bones from the badlands of the southwest of the state |
| **Wyoming** | Lance Formation | Bones and teeth from rock of the same age as Hell Creek |
| **Colorado** | Denver Formation | Teeth and bones from around the Denver area |
| **Utah** | North Horn Formation | Partial remains described in 2005, from much farther south than the classic sites |
| **New Mexico** | Hall Lake Formation | Remains named in 2024 as a possible older species, *Tyrannosaurus mcraeensis* (more on that below) |
| **Texas** | Javelina Formation | Jaw pieces and teeth from Big Bend National Park, the southernmost T. rex finds |
| **Alberta** | Scollard and Willow Creek formations | "Black Beauty", found in 1980, whose bones were stained dark by minerals |
| **Saskatchewan** | Frenchman Formation | "Scotty", found in 1991 near Eastend, the heaviest T. rex known |

If your state isn't on the list, that doesn't mean T. rex never walked there. It means the right age of rock isn't
exposed at the surface there, or nobody has found bones in it yet. Fossils only show up where 66-million-year-old
riverbeds have been worn open by erosion, and most of that land is in the dry badlands of the northern plains.

## North America was split in two

Look at a map of the Late Cretaceous and you'll barely recognize the continent. A long, shallow sea called the
**Western Interior Seaway** ran from the Arctic Ocean down to the Gulf of Mexico, right through the middle of what is now
the United States. At its widest, during most of the Late Cretaceous, it cut North America into two island continents:

- **Laramidia** in the west, a long strip running from Alaska to Mexico. This is where T. rex lived.
- **Appalachia** in the east, from the eastern Great Plains to the Atlantic coast.

By T. rex's time the seaway was shrinking and draining away to the north and south, but its shoreline still ran across
the Dakotas. The Hell Creek rocks were laid down by rivers flowing east off the young Rocky Mountains toward that sea.
So when you picture where T. rex lived, picture a coastal plain, not a mountain or a desert.

The east had its own big predators. *Appalachiosaurus* in Alabama and *Dryptosaurus* in New Jersey were tyrannosaur
relatives, but smaller and more lightly built. No T. rex fossil has ever been found east of the old seaway.

## What the T. rex habitat looked like

The Hell Creek rocks are some of the best-studied in the world, so we know a lot about the land T. rex walked. Picture
something closer to the low, swampy country of the Gulf Coast today than to modern Montana:

- **Rivers everywhere.** Wide, winding rivers with sandbars, ponds and backwater swamps spread across a flat plain.
  Most T. rex skeletons are found in old river channel sands, where bodies were washed in and buried.
- **Forests along the water.** Fossil leaves show flowering trees and shrubs (relatives of sycamores, laurels and
  magnolias), mixed with conifers, ferns, cycad-like plants and ginkgos. There was no grass yet.
- **Warm and wet.** Crocodilians, turtles and big amphibious reptiles lived there, which tells us the winters weren't
  harsh. It was warmer and far more humid than the northern plains are now.
- **The sea not far off.** To the east lay the shores of the seaway, with its sharks and marine reptiles.

That's a very different home from T. rex's feathered cousin *Yutyrannus*, which lived in a cool climate in China about
60 million years earlier. It's one reason scientists think a giant, warm-living T. rex didn't need a coat of fluff. Our
article on [whether T. rex had feathers](/blogs/blog/did-t-rex-have-feathers) covers that question.

## The Hell Creek Formation: T. rex's home ground

If you only remember one name, make it the **Hell Creek Formation**. It's a layer of mudstone and sandstone up to a few
hundred feet thick, spread across eastern Montana, the western Dakotas and a corner of Wyoming. It's named after Hell
Creek near Jordan, Montana, where Barnum Brown of the American Museum of Natural History dug up the first T. rex
skeleton in 1902. Henry Fairfield Osborn named the animal *Tyrannosaurus rex*, "tyrant lizard king", in 1905.

Hell Creek covers roughly the last two million years before the asteroid. At the top of the formation, in some places,
you can see the thin clay layer left by the impact 66 million years ago. Below that line there are dinosaurs. Above it,
there are none (except birds).

It's also one of the few places on Earth where you can still join a dig. Several museums and private outfits in
Montana and the Dakotas run paid fossil-dig days in summer, usually on private ranch land with the owner's permission.
Fossils on federal land belong to the public and can only be collected under a permit.

## Who lived next door

T. rex was the biggest predator in its world, but it wasn't alone. These animals all turn up in the same rocks:

| Neighbor | What it was | How it got along with T. rex |
|---|---|---|
| *Triceratops* | Three-horned plant-eater, up to about 30 feet long | The most common big dinosaur in Hell Creek, and a regular meal. Triceratops bones with T. rex bite marks have been found. |
| *Edmontosaurus* | A big duck-billed dinosaur | Prey. One Edmontosaurus tail has a healed T. rex bite, so at least one got away. |
| *Ankylosaurus* | The armored, club-tailed tank | Probably left alone. Rare in the rocks. |
| *Pachycephalosaurus* | Dome-headed plant-eater | Likely prey, though rarely found |
| *Thescelosaurus* | Small, quick plant-eater | Easy prey for a young T. rex |
| *Dakotaraptor*, *Acheroraptor* | Raptors, one human-sized and one smaller | Competitors for small prey, not a threat to adults |
| *Anzu* | A big, toothless, crested feathered dinosaur | Probably ate a bit of everything |
| Crocodilians, turtles, early mammals | Swamp and riverbank life | Mostly beneath T. rex's notice |

Young T. rex probably hunted smaller animals than their parents did, filling the role raptors play elsewhere. For what
T. rex actually ate, see our [T. rex diet guide](/blogs/blog/t-rex-diet-facts-what-did-tyrannosaurus-rex-really-eat).

## When did T. rex live?

*Tyrannosaurus rex* lived from about **68 to 66 million years ago**, in the last stage of the Cretaceous period. That's
a short run for such a famous animal, perhaps two to three million years. It ended when the asteroid hit what is now
Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula 66 million years ago. T. rex was one of the very last non-bird dinosaurs alive.

Two comparisons help put that in perspective:

- **T. rex lived closer in time to us than to *Stegosaurus*.** Stegosaurus lived about 150 million years ago, roughly 84
  million years before T. rex. T. rex is about 66 million years before us.
- **T. rex never met *Allosaurus*, *Brachiosaurus* or *Velociraptor*.** The first two were Jurassic animals, long
  gone. Velociraptor lived in Mongolia, and millions of years earlier.

## How many T. rex were there?

In 2021, a team led by Charles Marshall at the University of California, Berkeley tried to estimate it, using how big
T. rex was, how much land it could live on and how long it lived. Their best guess was about **20,000 adult T. rex
alive at any one time**, spread over a range of very roughly 2.3 million square kilometers. Over the whole life of the species, that adds up to
about **2.5 billion T. rex**.

The team was clear that the numbers come with wide error bars; the real total could be several times higher or lower.
But the order of magnitude is the interesting part. Billions of T. rex lived, and we have found a few dozen good
skeletons. Fossilization is rare.

## How did T. rex get to North America?

T. rex's closest known relative is *Tarbosaurus*, a slightly smaller tyrannosaur from Mongolia. The two look so alike
that some scientists have argued they belong in the same genus. That raises a question: did T. rex's family start in
Asia or in North America?

- **The Asia camp.** Several family-tree studies, including Brusatte and Carr's 2016 analysis, put T. rex closest to
  Asian tyrannosaurs. On that view, a *Tarbosaurus*-like ancestor walked across a land bridge between Siberia and
  Alaska, where the Bering Strait is now, and its descendants became T. rex. A 2025 study in *Royal Society Open
  Science* reached the same conclusion.
- **The southern North America camp.** In 2024, Sebastian Dalman and colleagues named *Tyrannosaurus mcraeensis* from
  New Mexico rocks a few million years older than T. rex. If they're right, the T. rex lineage was already in southern
  Laramidia before T. rex appeared, and may have spread north from there. Other paleontologists say the New Mexico bones
  aren't different enough from T. rex to be a new species, or that their age is uncertain.

The honest answer is that it's still argued. Either way, the T. rex we know, *Tyrannosaurus rex* itself, is only found
in western North America.

## Where you can see a real T. rex

You can't visit T. rex's home as it was, but you can see the animals that came out of it. A few famous ones:

- **Sue**, the Field Museum, Chicago. Found in South Dakota in 1990, about 90% complete.
- **The Nation's T. rex**, the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History, Washington, D.C. The Montana "Wankel
  rex", found in 1988 near Fort Peck Reservoir.
- **The first T. rex**, the Carnegie Museum of Natural History, Pittsburgh. Barnum Brown's 1902 Montana skeleton.
- **Scotty**, the T.rex Discovery Centre in Eastend, Saskatchewan, run by the Royal Saskatchewan Museum, near where it
  was found.
- **The Museum of the Rockies**, Bozeman, Montana, which has led many of the Hell Creek digs.

## FAQ

**Where did T. rex live?**
In western North America, from Alberta and Saskatchewan down through Montana, the Dakotas, Wyoming, Colorado and Utah
to New Mexico and Texas. The most fossils come from the Hell Creek Formation of Montana and the Dakotas.

**What continent did T. rex live on?**
North America. No T. rex fossils have been found on any other continent. Its close relative *Tarbosaurus* lived in
Asia.

**When did T. rex live?**
About 68 to 66 million years ago, at the end of the Cretaceous period. It died out with the other non-bird dinosaurs
when the asteroid hit.

**What was the T. rex habitat like?**
Warm, humid river plains with forests of flowering trees, conifers and ferns, near the shore of a shallow inland sea.
Crocodilians, turtles and *Triceratops* lived there too.

**Did T. rex live in the Jurassic?**
No. Despite the movie title, T. rex is a Cretaceous dinosaur. It lived roughly 80 million years after Jurassic animals
like *Stegosaurus* and *Allosaurus*.

**Did T. rex live in my state?**
If you live in the western Great Plains or the Rocky Mountain states, maybe. T. rex fossils have been found in Montana,
South Dakota, North Dakota, Wyoming, Colorado, Utah, New Mexico and Texas. East of the old inland sea, a different
group of smaller tyrannosaurs lived instead.

**Did T. rex live in Africa, Europe or South America?**
No. Africa and South America had their own big predators, such as *Spinosaurus* and *Giganotosaurus*, and both of them
lived millions of years before T. rex.

**Where was the first T. rex found?**
Near Jordan, Montana, in 1902, by Barnum Brown of the American Museum of Natural History. It's now at the Carnegie
Museum of Natural History in Pittsburgh.
