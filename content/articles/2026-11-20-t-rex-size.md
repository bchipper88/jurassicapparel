# T. Rex Size: How Long, How Tall and How Heavy Tyrannosaurus Really Was

Your kid has just asked how big a T. rex was, and "really big" didn't cut it. Here's the answer to the T. rex size
question in numbers you can picture: the biggest known *Tyrannosaurus rex* skeletons are about **40 feet (12–13 m)
long**, stood about **12 feet (3.7 m) tall at the hip**, and weighed an estimated **8 to 9.5 tonnes**, about as much
as a large African elephant and a half. That's roughly the length of a school bus, with a head higher than a basketball
hoop.

The rest of this page explains where those numbers come from, why you'll see "20 feet tall" in older books, how much
the estimates disagree, and how to make it real for a kid with a stick of chalk and a driveway.

## T. rex size at a glance

| Measurement | Big adult (Sue, Scotty) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Length**, snout to tail tip | About 40–43 ft (12–13 m) | Sue's mount at the Field Museum is about 40.5 ft (12.3 m) |
| **Height at the hip** | About 12–13 ft (3.7–4 m) | The usual "how tall" figure |
| **Height of the head**, walking | Roughly 12–15 ft | It held its head about level with its back, not raised high |
| **Weight** | About 8–9.5 tonnes (17,600–21,000 lb) | Every weight is a model estimate, not a weighing |
| **Skull length** | About 5 ft (1.5 m) | See our [T. rex skull guide](/blogs/blog/t-rex-skull) |
| **Largest tooth** (with root) | About 12 in (30 cm) | See our [guide to T. rex teeth](/blogs/blog/t-rex-teeth) |
| **Arm length** | About 3 ft (1 m) | Short for the body, but heavily muscled |
| **Age at full size** | Around 20 years | Sue was about 28 when she died (Erickson et al. 2004) |

Those are figures for the largest skeletons we have. A typical adult was smaller, and a teenager was much smaller.

## How long was T. rex?

The most complete big skeleton, **Sue** at the Field Museum in Chicago, is mounted at about **40.5 feet (12.3 m)**
from the tip of the snout to the tip of the tail. **Scotty**, at the Royal Saskatchewan Museum, is thought to have been
a little longer. The 2019 study that described it in detail (Persons and colleagues) put it at about **43 feet
(13 m)**.

Almost half of that length is tail. The tail wasn't dead weight. It balanced the huge head over the hips like a seesaw,
and it anchored the biggest leg muscle, the *caudofemoralis*, which pulled the thigh back with each step. Without the
tail, T. rex would have tipped onto its face.

For scale:

- A full-size yellow school bus is usually **35–45 feet** long. A big T. rex is about one bus.
- A standard bowling lane is 60 feet. T. rex covers two-thirds of it.
- A large saltwater crocodile is around 17–20 feet. You'd need two, nose to tail, to match one T. rex.

## How tall was T. rex?

This is where the numbers get confusing, so it's worth taking slowly.

**At the hip, a big T. rex stood about 12 to 13 feet (3.7–4 m) tall.** That's the measurement most scientists mean
when they say how tall it was, because the hip is a fixed point on the skeleton. Sue's hip sits about 12 feet off the
floor.

**The head was not much higher than the hips when it walked.** T. rex carried its body roughly level, like a giant
bird, with its head forward and its tail straight out behind. With the head held about level with the back, the top
of the skull was somewhere around 12 to 15 feet off the ground. If it lifted its head, it could reach higher, but it
didn't stand around like that.

**Why older books say 18 to 20 feet tall.** For most of the 20th century, museums mounted T. rex upright, like a
kangaroo, with the tail dragging on the floor. In that pose the head reached about 18–20 feet. The American Museum of
Natural History's famous mount stood that way for decades before it was remounted in a horizontal pose in the 1990s.
Footprints and trackways from big theropods show no tail drags, and the joints of the hips and backbone don't fit an
upright stance well. The upright T. rex is gone, but the "20 feet tall" figure still turns up in toy boxes and
quizzes.

How that compares with things you know:

| Thing | Height | Next to T. rex |
|---|---|---|
| An adult person | about 5 ft 9 in (1.75 m) | You'd stand roughly at its knee and see its belly well above your head |
| A basketball hoop | 10 ft (3 m) | Shorter than T. rex's hip |
| An African elephant bull | about 10–13 ft at the shoulder | About the same height as T. rex's hip |
| A single-storey house, eaves | about 10–12 ft | T. rex could look onto the roof |
| A giraffe | up to about 18 ft | Taller, but weighs well under a fifth as much |

## How much did T. rex weigh?

Nobody weighed one, so every number here comes from a model. The best estimates for the biggest skeletons fall around
**8 to 9.5 tonnes**, which is about **17,600 to 21,000 pounds**. Sue's estimates mostly sit at the top of that range.
Scotty's 2019 description gave about **8.9 tonnes (19,500 lb)**.

To picture that: a large African elephant weighs about 6 tonnes. A big T. rex weighed about one and a half elephants,
or roughly as much as four or five mid-size cars.

### Two ways scientists estimate the weight

1. **From the leg bones.** Animals that walk on their legs need thicker leg bones the heavier they are. Measure the
   distance around the thigh bone (femur) and the shin bone, compare with living animals, and you get a weight. It's
   quick and it works on incomplete skeletons, but it has a wide margin of error.
2. **From a 3D model of the whole body.** Scan the skeleton, wrap it in a digital body with muscle, organs and air
   sacs, and calculate the volume. Multiply by a density and you get a weight. John Hutchinson and colleagues did this
   for Sue and other tyrannosaurs in 2011. The answer depends on how fat or lean you make the model, which is why
   the same skeleton can get estimates thousands of pounds apart.

Both methods land in the same general area for a big adult. That agreement is the reason the 8–9.5 tonne figure is
trusted, even though no single number is certain.

## How do we know how big T. rex was?

Partly by measuring, partly by filling gaps, and partly by statistics.

- **Measuring bones.** Length and hip height come straight off the skeleton. Sue is about 90% complete by volume,
  so there's little guessing.
- **Filling gaps.** Most T. rex skeletons are missing pieces, often tail bones. Missing parts are sized from more
  complete skeletons, which adds a little uncertainty to every length.
- **Counting growth rings.** Bones grow in yearly layers, a bit like tree rings. Gregory Erickson's team counted them
  in 2004 and found that T. rex grew slowly for its first few years, then hit a **teenage growth spurt** in which it
  put on roughly **4–5 pounds (about 2 kg) a day**. Growth slowed in its late teens, and it was fully grown at
  about 20.
- **Statistics.** We have only a few dozen decent T. rex skeletons out of the many thousands of animals that lived.
  The chance that one of them is the biggest that ever lived is small. In 2024, Jordan Mallon and David Hone modelled
  what the largest T. rex in the population might have been. Their estimate was about **15 tonnes and around 50 feet
  (15 m)**, roughly **70% heavier** than the biggest skeleton we have. That's a statistical estimate, not a fossil.
  Nobody has found that animal. It's the story behind the "T. rex could have been much bigger" headlines.

## How big was a baby or young T. rex?

Much smaller than you'd guess. A hatchling was probably about the size of a turkey, and a two- or three-year-old
might have been the size of a large dog.

The best-known teenager is **Jane**, at the Burpee Museum in Rockford, Illinois. Jane was about **21 feet (6.5 m)
long** and around 11 or 12 years old when it died, about half the length of Sue and a small fraction of the weight.
Jane had long legs and a narrow, shallow skull. Young T. rex were built for speed, and the heavy, bone-crushing build
came later. Whether Jane is a young T. rex or a separate, smaller animal called *Nanotyrannus* is still argued over;
we covered that in the [skull guide](/blogs/blog/t-rex-skull).

So "how big was T. rex" really depends on which T. rex you mean. A 10-year-old T. rex and a 28-year-old one were as
different in size as a pony and an elephant.

## Was T. rex the biggest dinosaur?

No, not even close, though it was among the biggest meat-eaters that ever walked on land.

| Dinosaur | Length | Weight (estimated) | Compared with T. rex |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Tyrannosaurus rex* | 40–43 ft | 8–9.5 t | — |
| *Giganotosaurus* | about 40–43 ft | usually estimated a little lighter | About the same length, slimmer build, smaller bite |
| *Spinosaurus* | up to about 45–50 ft in some estimates | debated | Possibly longer, but lighter and built for water |
| *Triceratops* | about 26–30 ft | about 6–12 t | Shorter, but a big one was in T. rex's weight class |
| *Argentinosaurus* | about 100–115 ft | around 70 t or more | Several times longer and roughly eight times heavier |

The long-necked sauropods were in a different league. Our post on
[the biggest dinosaur ever](/blogs/blog/the-biggest-dinosaur-ever-meet-the-titans-that-dwarfed-everything) covers
them. Among meat-eaters, T. rex was likely the heaviest and most robust we know of, even if one or two rivals were
a little longer.

## T. rex vs you, the elephant and the school bus

If you want one comparison to remember:

- **Length:** one school bus.
- **Hip height:** an elephant's shoulder, or a bit more than a basketball hoop.
- **Weight:** about one and a half elephants.
- **Head:** about 5 feet long, as long as a small adult is tall.
- **Your height:** about knee-high to it.

## Make T. rex life-size: a driveway activity

Numbers stay abstract until you see them on the ground. This takes about 15 minutes and a tape measure.

1. **Mark the length.** On a driveway, playground or quiet sidewalk, measure out **40 feet** with a tape measure, or
   count it in steps (most adults take about 2.5 feet per step, so about 16 steps). Chalk a line.
2. **Mark the skull.** Chalk a 5-foot box at one end. Have your kid lie down inside it. Most children fit
   completely inside the head.
3. **Mark the hip.** About 15–16 feet back from the snout, draw a big dot. That's roughly where the legs were.
4. **Show the height.** Stand next to a wall and mark 12 feet with a ladder or a long pole held up against it, or
   point to the top of a basketball backboard. That's hip height.
5. **Walk it.** Walk the line from nose to tail. Count the steps.

It's the best answer to "how big was T. rex" we know. It also works in a classroom hallway.

## FAQ

**How big was a T. rex?**
The biggest known skeletons are about 40–43 feet (12–13 m) long, about 12–13 feet (3.7–4 m) tall at the hip, and
estimated at 8 to 9.5 tonnes. That's about the length of a school bus and the weight of one and a half elephants.

**How tall was T. rex?**
About 12 to 13 feet at the hip. It carried its head roughly level with its back, so the top of its head was around
12 to 15 feet up when walking. The "20 feet tall" figure comes from old, upright museum mounts that scientists no
longer think are right.

**How much did a T. rex weigh?**
Big adults are estimated at about 8 to 9.5 tonnes (about 17,600 to 21,000 lb). Scotty was estimated at about
8.9 tonnes in 2019. All weights are model estimates.

**What is the biggest T. rex ever found?**
Scotty, in Saskatchewan, is usually described as the heaviest known, at about 43 feet long. Sue, in Chicago, is the
most complete large skeleton and is about 40.5 feet long. Which is "biggest" depends on how you measure.

**Could T. rex have been even bigger?**
Probably, yes. A 2024 study estimated that the largest T. rex alive at any time may have been about 70% heavier than
the biggest skeleton we have, perhaps around 15 tonnes. No such fossil has been found.

**How big was T. rex compared to a human?**
An average adult would stand around its knee. Its hip was about twice your height, and its skull alone was about as
long as a small adult is tall.

**Was T. rex bigger than Spinosaurus or Giganotosaurus?**
It was probably heavier and more strongly built than both. *Spinosaurus* may have been longer, and *Giganotosaurus*
was about the same length. Estimates for both are less certain because their skeletons are less complete.
