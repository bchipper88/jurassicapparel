# T. Rex Teeth: How Many It Had, How Big They Got, and How to Spot a Real Fossil Tooth

Somebody at your house has just asked how big a T. rex tooth was, and "really big" didn't satisfy
them. Here is the full answer. T. rex teeth were the largest of any meat-eating dinosaur we know
well. The biggest ones were about a foot long once you count the root. A Tyrannosaurus had roughly
50 to 60 of them at any one time, and it never stopped growing new ones. They weren't built like
steak knives. They were thick, rounded spikes, made for biting through bone instead of slicing flesh.

Below: the numbers, why the teeth were shaped that way, what fossils show they were used for, a
comparison with other animals, quick facts for kids, and what to check before you buy a fossil tooth.

## T. rex teeth at a glance

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| How many teeth? | About 50–60 at once; the exact count varies from skull to skull |
| Biggest tooth? | About 12 inches (30 cm) including the root |
| Shape? | Thick and rounded, nicknamed "killer bananas", with serrated edges |
| Did they grow back? | Yes, all its life; a new tooth grew in beneath each old one |
| Could it bite through bone? | Yes. Fossil bite marks and fossil dung both show it |
| Did they show when its mouth was shut? | Probably not; a 2023 study argues for lips |

## How many teeth did T. rex have?

A grown T. rex had **somewhere between 50 and 60 teeth** in its mouth at once. You won't find
one exact number, for two reasons. Different skulls preserve different counts, and at any moment
some sockets held a tooth that had just fallen out or one that was still coming in.

They weren't all the same:

- **At the very front of the upper jaw** sat a small row of teeth (four on each side) that were
  D-shaped in cross-section: rounded on the outside and flatter on the inside. They worked more
  like a set of incisors, good for gripping and scraping meat off bone.
- **Along the sides of both jaws** were the big teeth people picture: tall, thick and curved
  slightly backward. The largest were in the upper jaw.
- **The sizes were mixed.** Old teeth, half-grown teeth and brand-new teeth sat side by side, so
  a T. rex smile looked jagged, not neat.

## How big were T. rex teeth?

The largest T. rex tooth found so far is about **12 inches (30 cm) long**. Most of that length is
root, not crown. T. rex had very long roots set deep in the jaw, which anchored each tooth against
the force of a bite. The crown, the part that stuck out of the gums, was smaller, but the biggest
crowns were still about the size of a banana. That's where the "killer bananas" nickname comes from.

For scale: an adult human canine is about an inch long, root included. Lay one next to the biggest
T. rex tooth and the dinosaur's is roughly twelve times longer and many times thicker.

## Why were T. rex teeth so thick?

Most meat-eating dinosaurs, Allosaurus for example, had teeth like flattened blades: thin from side
to side and good for slicing. T. rex teeth were much **thicker from side to side**, close to round
in cross-section at the base. Thickness gives strength. A thin blade snaps when it hits bone; a
thick spike punches through.

Three details made them work:

1. **Serrated edges.** The front and back edges carried rows of tiny bumps called denticles, like the
   teeth on a bread knife. They gripped meat and helped tear it.
2. **Built-in crack stoppers.** A 2015 study found that the base of each serration had a small
   chamber, and the tissue around it was arranged to stop cracks from spreading. The teeth could
   take punishment without splitting.
3. **Deep roots.** As above: the tooth sat deep in the jaw, so it didn't wrench out on a struggling
   animal.

## What could T. rex teeth do? The fossil evidence

We don't have to guess. Several fossils show T. rex teeth at work:

- **Bite marks on bone.** A Triceratops hip bone has dozens of tooth punctures. Researchers matched
  them to T. rex, and some go deep into solid bone.
- **A tooth stuck in a living animal.** An Edmontosaurus (a duck-billed dinosaur) tail from South
  Dakota has the broken tip of a tyrannosaur tooth lodged in it. The bone had healed around it, so
  the Edmontosaurus survived the bite. That's strong evidence T. rex attacked live prey and didn't
  only scavenge.
- **Bone in its dung.** A very large fossil dropping from Saskatchewan, thought to be from T. rex,
  is packed with crushed bone fragments. Whatever made it chewed through bone and swallowed it.

The bite behind those teeth was enormous. A widely cited 2017 study estimated it at about
**35,000 newtons at the back teeth, roughly 7,800 pounds of force**. Other estimates run higher.
T. rex seems to have fed by "puncture and pull": bite down hard, then rip backward, letting the
serrations do the tearing. For what it was actually eating, see our guide to
[what T. rex really ate](/blogs/blog/t-rex-diet-facts-what-did-tyrannosaurus-rex-really-eat).

## Did T. rex teeth grow back?

Yes, all the time. Like crocodiles and sharks, and unlike us, T. rex replaced its teeth throughout
its life. A new tooth formed in the jaw beneath each working tooth. When the old one wore down or
broke off, the replacement moved up into place.

It wasn't fast by dinosaur standards. One study counted the daily growth lines inside fossil
teeth, which work like tree rings. It estimated that a T. rex tooth took **around two years** to be
replaced. Some long-necked plant-eaters were far quicker. Nigersaurus, with about 500 teeth,
seems to have replaced each one every couple of weeks.

All that replacing is why shed T. rex teeth are among the most common tyrannosaur fossils.
Each animal dropped many teeth over its life, and every one of them could end up buried.

## Did T. rex have lips over its teeth?

The movie T. rex shows off its teeth with its mouth shut. The real one probably didn't. A 2023
study in *Science* compared dinosaur skulls and tooth enamel with living reptiles. It concluded
that T. rex and its relatives likely had lip-like tissue covering their teeth when the mouth was
closed, much as lizards do. Enamel dries out and wears quickly if it's always exposed, and T. rex
enamel doesn't show that kind of wear.

Not every paleontologist agrees, and some think crocodile-style exposed teeth fit the evidence
too. But the "lips" view has a lot of support right now. If you draw a resting T. rex with its
teeth hidden, you're on solid ground.

## T. rex teeth compared with other animals

| Animal | Teeth | Shape | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tyrannosaurus rex** | ~50–60 | Thick, rounded, serrated | Biting through bone |
| Allosaurus | Dozens | Thin, flat, serrated blades | Slicing flesh |
| Spinosaurus | Dozens | Straight cones, little or no serration | Gripping slippery fish |
| Triceratops | Hundreds (in stacked "dental batteries") | Packed together into a cutting edge | Shearing tough plants |
| Edmontosaurus | Hundreds (dental batteries) | Packed together into a grinding surface | Grinding plants |
| Nigersaurus | ~500 | Small, in a wide straight row | Cropping low plants |
| Great white shark | ~300, in rows | Flat, triangular, serrated | Slicing chunks of flesh |
| Adult human | 32 | Mixed | Everything |

So T. rex didn't have the most teeth. The plant-eaters beat it easily. What it had were the biggest,
toughest teeth of any land predator we know.

## What a real T. rex tooth looks like (and how to tell if one is real)

Shed T. rex teeth are sold legally in the US. Most come from rock layers in Montana, the Dakotas
and Wyoming, such as the Hell Creek and Lance formations. That's what the shops at the top of
search results are selling. We don't sell fossils or casts, but if you're thinking of buying, here's
what to ask first:

- **Where was it found?** A good dealer names the formation and the state. In the US, vertebrate
  fossils from federal land can only be collected with a permit and stay in public collections,
  so a legally sold tooth should come from private land, collected with the owner's permission.
- **Has it been restored?** Many fossil teeth are repaired, with cracks filled or a broken tip
  rebuilt and painted. That's normal, but it should be disclosed and it affects the value. Ask
  what percentage is original.
- **Is it definitely T. rex?** Other meat-eaters lived alongside T. rex, and teeth from young
  tyrannosaurs and from close relatives can look alike. Scientists are still debating some of them
  (whether "Nanotyrannus" is a separate animal, for one). A careful dealer explains how a tooth was
  identified. A vague label, with a price that seems too good, is a warning sign.
- **Is it a cast?** Resin replicas are great for kids and classrooms and cost a fraction of the
  real thing, but they should be sold as replicas. Real fossil teeth feel heavy and stone-cold.
  Their surface usually shows fine cracks and color variation from the rock they were buried in.

## T. rex teeth FAQ

**How many teeth did a T. rex have?**
About 50 to 60 at any one time. The count varies between skulls, and some sockets were always
between teeth because T. rex replaced them throughout its life.

**How big was the biggest T. rex tooth?**
About 12 inches (30 cm) including the root. The visible crown was shorter, but the largest were
still banana-sized.

**Were T. rex teeth sharp?**
They had sharp tips and serrated edges, but they weren't thin and razor-like. They were thick and
strong, built to punch through bone rather than slice.

**Could T. rex crush bone?**
Yes. Bite marks deep in Triceratops bone, and a fossil dropping full of bone fragments, both show
it did. Estimates put its bite force at around 35,000 newtons or more.

**Did T. rex lose its teeth?**
Constantly, and that was normal. A replacement grew in beneath each tooth, taking about two years.
That's why shed teeth are some of the most common T. rex fossils.

**Did T. rex have lips?**
Probably. A 2023 study argues its teeth were covered by lip-like tissue when its mouth was closed,
though not every scientist agrees yet.

**Can you buy a real T. rex tooth?**
Yes. In the US, shed teeth collected legally on private land are sold by fossil dealers. Ask for
the locality, the formation, how much has been restored, and how the tooth was identified.
