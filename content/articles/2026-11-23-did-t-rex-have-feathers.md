# Did T. Rex Have Feathers? What the Fossils Say About a Feathered Tyrannosaurus Rex

Maybe a new dinosaur book showed a fluffy T. rex and your kid said "that's wrong," or a museum model looked nothing
like the one from the movies. So: **did T. rex have feathers?** The best answer from the fossils is **mostly no**. The
few patches of *Tyrannosaurus rex* skin ever found show small, pebbly scales. But its family is full of feathered
animals, including a 30-foot cousin covered in long fuzz. So a **feathered Tyrannosaurus rex** isn't a crazy idea. It
just isn't what the T. rex fossils show.

The question isn't settled, and the reasons are interesting. Below: the feathered relatives, the scaly skin, the parts
of a T. rex nobody has seen yet, and the most likely look on the evidence we have today.

## The short answer

| Question | What the evidence says | How sure |
|---|---|---|
| Did T. rex's relatives have feathers? | Yes. *Dilong* and *Yutyrannus*, both early tyrannosaurs from China, are preserved with filament-like feathers. | Very sure |
| What does T. rex skin look like? | Small scales, from patches on the neck, hip and tail of one Montana specimen. | Sure, for those spots |
| Was an adult T. rex covered in feathers? | Probably not. Its big cousins also show scales, and a giant in a warm climate didn't need a coat. | Fairly sure |
| Could it have had *some* feathers? | Possibly, on its back, where no skin has been found. | Open question |
| Were baby T. rex fuzzy? | Nobody knows. No baby T. rex skin has ever been found. | Pure guesswork |
| Did T. rex have wing feathers? | No. Its relatives had simple fuzzy strands, not the stiff, vaned feathers of a bird's wing. | Very sure |

## Why scientists expected a feathered T. rex

For most of the 20th century, every dinosaur was drawn scaly, like a giant lizard. That changed in 1996, when a small
predator from northeast China called *Sinosauropteryx* turned up with a fringe of fine, hair-like filaments along its
back and tail. It was the first dinosaur found with anything like feathers. Hundreds more followed from the same rocks.

The striking thing is *where* those animals sit on the family tree. T. rex belongs to the **coelurosaurs**, the branch
of meat-eating dinosaurs that also includes *Velociraptor*, *Sinosauropteryx* and, eventually, birds. Fuzz and feathers
turn up all over that branch. When nearly everyone in a family has a trait, the safe bet is that the family's common
ancestor had it too. So in the early 2000s, a lot of scientists expected T. rex to turn out fuzzy as well.

Then two of its own relatives made the case stronger.

## Dilong: the small feathered tyrannosaur

In 2004, Xu Xing and his colleagues described *Dilong paradoxus* ("paradoxical emperor dragon") from rocks about 126
million years old in Liaoning, China. It was an early member of the tyrannosaur family, about 5 feet (1.6 m) long,
light enough to pick up.

*Dilong* was preserved with simple filaments, branched strands up to roughly 2 cm (just under an inch) long, near its
jaw and tail. It was the first direct proof that at least some tyrannosaurs had fuzz. The obvious objection: *Dilong*
was tiny. Small animals lose body heat fast, which is why mice have fur. A small, fuzzy tyrannosaur doesn't tell you
much about a 9-ton one.

## Yutyrannus: the big feathered tyrannosaur

Eight years later, the same team answered that objection. In 2012 they described *Yutyrannus huali*, which means
"beautiful feathered tyrant." It came from the same region, about 125 million years old, and it was no lightweight.
The largest of the three skeletons was about **30 feet (9 m) long** and roughly 1.4 tonnes. It's still the biggest
animal ever found with direct evidence of feathers.

Its filaments were long, about 15 to 20 cm (6 to 8 inches) in places, and they were found on several parts of the
body. So *Yutyrannus* was a big predator wearing a shaggy coat. That's exactly the picture that makes people ask about
T. rex.

There's a catch, and it matters. Studies of the rocks *Yutyrannus* lived in suggest a cool climate, with a mean annual
temperature of around 10 °C (50 °F) (Amiot et al. 2011). That's closer to Seattle than to Miami. A big animal in a
chilly forest has a good reason to keep a coat. You can read more about this animal in our
[Yutyrannus fact file](/blogs/dinosaur-facts/yutyrannus).

One more point that gets lost in the headlines: *Yutyrannus* is a distant cousin of T. rex, not its grandparent. It
lived about **60 million years before T. rex**, which is roughly the same gap as between T. rex and us.

## The T. rex skin fossils: scales, not feathers

Here's what actually turned up when people looked at T. rex itself.

Skin almost never fossilizes, and most T. rex skeletons have none at all. But a specimen from Montana nicknamed
**"Wyrex"** (now at the Houston Museum of Natural Science) preserved small patches of skin impressions from the
**neck, the hip and the base of the tail**. In 2017, a team led by Phil Bell published those patches, along with skin
from four other big tyrannosaurs: *Albertosaurus*, *Daspletosaurus*, *Gorgosaurus* and *Tarbosaurus*.

Every patch showed the same thing: **small, non-overlapping scales**, a bit like the pebbly skin on a Gila monster or on
a chicken's lower leg. There were no feathers, and no sign that feathers had ever grown there.

Bell's team drew a straightforward conclusion. The big tyrannosaurs were scaly over large parts of the body, and if
they had feathers at all, those were probably limited to the back. In other words, the feathered ancestors had passed
down a scaly giant. (There's another idea, in which tyrannosaurs had feathers that were later "lost," but the result is
the same.)

### Why the question isn't completely closed

The skin patches are small. Together they cover a tiny fraction of a 40-foot animal, and **none of them comes from the
back**, which is exactly where a strip of feathers would most likely be. Modern birds also mix scales and feathers on
one body. A chicken has a scaly foot and a feathered leg a few inches above it. So the honest summary is:

- We've seen T. rex skin in a handful of spots, and it's scaly in all of them.
- We haven't seen the back, the top of the head, or a baby.
- Anything drawn on those parts is an educated guess.

## Why a giant T. rex probably didn't need a feather coat

There's a good physical reason to expect a mostly bare adult, and you can see it at the zoo.

As an animal gets bigger, its volume grows much faster than its skin area. A huge body makes plenty of heat and has
relatively little surface to lose it through. That's why elephants, rhinos and hippos are nearly hairless. Their
problem is staying *cool*, not warm. The woolly mammoth kept its coat because it lived through ice ages.

T. rex was enormous, up to about 40 feet long and 8 to 9 tonnes (our [T. rex size guide](/blogs/blog/t-rex-size)
explains where those figures come from). It lived about 68 to 66 million years ago in what's now Montana, the Dakotas,
Wyoming and western Canada, when the climate there was far warmer than today. A thick feather coat on a nine-tonne
animal in that setting would have been like an elephant wearing a parka. *Yutyrannus* fits the same rule from the other
side. It was big, but it lived somewhere cold.

## What about baby T. rex?

This is the part most likely to change in a museum near you over the next few years, and it's also the part with the
least evidence.

A newly hatched tyrannosaur was small. Embryonic tyrannosaur bones from Alberta and Montana, described in 2021 by
Greg Funston and colleagues, suggest hatchlings were around **3 feet (0.9 m) long**. Small animals lose heat quickly,
and fuzz is an ancestral trait in this family. So many artists now draw T. rex chicks with a downy coat that they lose
as they grow, the way some birds trade chick fluff for adult feathers.

It's a reasonable idea, but no fossil supports it yet. **No skin from a baby or juvenile T. rex has ever been found.**
If you see a fluffy baby T. rex in a documentary, treat it as a good guess.

## So what did T. rex actually look like?

Putting it all together, here's the reconstruction most paleontologists would sign off on today:

1. **Mostly scaly skin**, with small pebbly scales over the neck, hips and tail at least.
2. **Maybe a sparse strip of filaments on the back.** It's possible, not proven, and it would look more like a coarse
   mane than a bird's plumage.
3. **Lips over the teeth.** This is a separate change from the old movie look. A 2023 study in *Science* (Cullen et
   al.) argued that big theropods had lip-like tissue covering their teeth when the mouth was closed, much like a
   monitor lizard. Our [T. rex skull guide](/blogs/blog/t-rex-skull) covers the bones underneath.
4. **No wing feathers.** Even its feathered relatives had simple strands, not the stiff, vaned feathers of a bird's
   wing.
5. **Color unknown.** Pigment has been read from some small feathered dinosaurs, but not from T. rex skin.

The giant fluffy "big chicken" T. rex that went viral in the 2010s was a fair guess at the time. The 2017 skin study is
what moved the consensus back toward scales.

## Why the pictures keep changing

If the models you grew up with look nothing like today's, here's the timeline:

- **1900s–1980s:** an upright, tail-dragging lizard, as in old museum mounts and monster movies.
- **1990s:** a horizontal, active, scaly predator, as in *Jurassic Park* (1993). The posture was right; the skin was a
  guess that turned out roughly right too.
- **2000s–2012:** feathered relatives (*Dilong*, then *Yutyrannus*) start a wave of feathered T. rex art and toys.
- **2017:** T. rex skin patches show scales, and the pendulum swings back.
- **Today:** mostly scaly adults, often with a few filaments on the back. Apple TV+'s *Prehistoric Planet* (2022)
  is a good example: a largely scaly adult with sparse fuzz, and downy chicks.

None of this means the scientists keep getting it wrong. Each new fossil narrows the guesswork. The skeleton of T. rex
has been well known for a century; T. rex skin was only described in print in 2017.

## Feathered T. rex facts for kids

1. The first dinosaur found with fuzz, *Sinosauropteryx*, was dug up in China and described in 1996.
2. T. rex's cousin *Yutyrannus* was about as long as a school bus and covered in fuzzy strands up to 8 inches long.
3. *Yutyrannus* lived in a chilly forest, so its fuzz probably kept it warm.
4. T. rex lived somewhere much warmer and was much bigger, so it didn't need a fuzzy coat.
5. Real T. rex skin fossils show little bumpy scales, like a chicken's foot.
6. Scientists have only found T. rex skin from its neck, hips and tail. Nobody knows for sure what its back looked like.
7. Baby T. rex might have been fluffy, but no baby T. rex skin has ever been found.
8. T. rex lived about 60 million years after *Yutyrannus*. That's about as long as from T. rex to you.
9. Birds are living dinosaurs. The closest living relatives of T. rex are birds and crocodiles.
10. Nobody knows what color T. rex was. Any color you pick could be right.

## FAQ

**Did T. rex have feathers?**
Probably not over most of its body. Skin fossils from its neck, hip and tail show small scales. It may have had some
filaments on its back, where no skin has been found, and its babies may have been fuzzy, but neither has been shown.

**Was T. rex related to feathered dinosaurs?**
Yes. Its relatives *Dilong* and *Yutyrannus* had filament-like feathers, and T. rex belongs to the coelurosaurs, the
group that includes birds.

**What was the biggest feathered dinosaur?**
*Yutyrannus huali*, at about 30 feet (9 m) long and about 1.4 tonnes. It's the largest animal found with direct
evidence of feathers.

**What did T. rex skin look like?**
Small, pebbly, non-overlapping scales, from the "Wyrex" specimen described in 2017 by Bell and colleagues.

**Is Jurassic Park's T. rex accurate?**
The scaly skin is closer to the evidence than the fluffy version that was popular in the 2010s. The movie T. rex lacks
lips, and its roar is invented.

**Did baby T. rex have feathers?**
Nobody knows. Many scientists think it's plausible because small animals lose heat faster, but no juvenile T. rex skin
has been found.

**Are birds related to T. rex?**
Yes. Birds are living dinosaurs that descend from small coelurosaurs. T. rex wasn't their ancestor; it was more like a
very distant cousin. Birds and crocodiles are its closest living relatives.
