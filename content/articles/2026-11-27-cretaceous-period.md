# The Cretaceous Period: 79 Million Years of Flowers, Seaways and the Last Dinosaurs

Most of the dinosaurs people can name lived in the **Cretaceous Period**. *Tyrannosaurus*, *Triceratops*,
*Velociraptor*, *Spinosaurus*, *Ankylosaurus*: all Cretaceous. So is the first flower, the first bee, and the giant
sea lizards that hunted across a sea covering the middle of North America.

It was also enormous. The Cretaceous ran from about 145 million years ago to 66 million years ago, roughly 79 million
years, which makes it the longest geologic period since animals with hard parts appeared. The world at the start of it
and the world at the end were almost different planets.

This guide walks through the whole thing: where the name comes from, what the continents and climate were doing, how
flowering plants took over, which dinosaurs lived when, what else was alive, and how it all ended.

## The Cretaceous Period at a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **When** | About 145 to 66 million years ago |
| **How long** | Roughly 79 million years, the longest period of the last 540 million |
| **Era** | The third and last period of the Mesozoic Era (after the Triassic and Jurassic) |
| **Divisions** | Early Cretaceous (about 145 to 100.5 million years ago) and Late Cretaceous (100.5 to 66 million years ago) |
| **Named for** | Chalk: Latin *creta*. Named in 1822 after the chalk beds of the Paris Basin |
| **Climate** | Warm greenhouse world; no permanent ice caps for most of it; forests near both poles |
| **Sea level** | Far higher than today; shallow seas flooded much of the continents |
| **Big events** | Pangaea finishes breaking up; flowering plants appear and spread; the Western Interior Seaway splits North America |
| **How it ended** | The Chicxulub asteroid impact, 66 million years ago, and the extinction of all non-bird dinosaurs |

## Why is it called the Cretaceous?

The name means "chalky." In 1822 the Belgian geologist Jean Baptiste Julius d'Omalius d'Halloy grouped the chalk beds
of the Paris Basin into a unit he called the *Terrain Crétacé*, from the Latin *creta*, chalk. The White Cliffs of
Dover are the same rock.

That chalk is made almost entirely of the microscopic plates of coccolithophores, single-celled algae that bloomed in
the warm, high seas of the Late Cretaceous. Their skeletons rained down for millions of years and piled up hundreds of
meters thick. When you see the cliffs, you're looking at a sea floor built of plankton.

The chalk also explains a puzzle on geology charts: the Cretaceous is abbreviated **K**, not C. That comes from
*Kreide*, the German word for chalk, and it keeps the Cretaceous from being confused with the Cambrian and
Carboniferous. It's why the end of the period is called the [K-Pg boundary](/blogs/blog/k-pg-boundary).

## When did the Cretaceous Period start and end?

The end is one of the best-dated moments in Earth's history: about 66.0 million years ago, fixed by the asteroid
debris layer found worldwide.

The start is surprisingly fuzzy. The Jurassic–Cretaceous boundary is the only boundary between periods in the last
half-billion years that still has no agreed "golden spike," the single reference rock section geologists use to define
a boundary. There was no mass extinction or sharp global event at that moment to mark it, so geologists are still
choosing between markers. The usual figure is about 145 million years ago; the official chart has recently nudged it a
little younger. Either way, you're safe saying "about 145 million."

The period is split in two, and each half into six ages (called stages). You'll see the stage names on museum labels
and in species posts:

| Half | Stages, oldest to youngest | Roughly |
|---|---|---|
| **Early Cretaceous** | Berriasian, Valanginian, Hauterivian, Barremian, Aptian, Albian | 145 to 100.5 million years ago |
| **Late Cretaceous** | Cenomanian, Turonian, Coniacian, Santonian, Campanian, Maastrichtian | 100.5 to 66 million years ago |

The two you'll meet most often are the **Campanian** (about 84 to 72 million years ago), when the badlands of Alberta
and Montana were laid down, and the **Maastrichtian** (72 to 66 million years ago), the final age, home of *T. rex* and
*Triceratops*.

## What did the Earth look like?

### The continents pulled apart

At the start of the Cretaceous, the supercontinent Pangaea had already split into a northern half (Laurasia) and a
southern half (Gondwana). Over the next 79 million years the pieces kept moving:

- **South America and Africa separated**, and the South Atlantic opened up between them in the Early Cretaceous.
- **India broke away** from Madagascar in the Late Cretaceous and began its long trip north toward Asia.
- **Australia and Antarctica** were still joined, and sat much farther south than Australia does now.
- **North America** was split down the middle by a sea (more below).

That breakup matters for dinosaurs. As the land masses separated, their animals evolved separately. By the Late
Cretaceous, the big predators of South America (abelisaurs like *Carnotaurus*) were a completely different family
from the tyrannosaurs of North America and Asia.

### The seas were very high

Cretaceous sea levels were among the highest of the last half-billion years, by most estimates well over 100 meters
above today's. Warm, shallow seas spread across the interiors of the continents, and they're why so much of the
Cretaceous record is marine rock: chalk, shale and limestone full of ammonites, clams and sea reptiles.

### The Western Interior Seaway

The most famous of those seas cut North America in half. From roughly 100 million years ago until near the end of the
period, the **Western Interior Seaway** ran from the Gulf of Mexico to the Arctic, across what is now Kansas, Nebraska,
the Dakotas and Alberta. It was shallow, warm and full of life: mosasaurs, long-necked plesiosaurs, giant turtles and
sharks.

West of it was a long, narrow landmass now called **Laramidia**, which is where almost every famous North American
dinosaur was found, from *Parasaurolophus* in Utah to *T. rex* in Montana. East of it was **Appalachia**, whose
dinosaurs are much less known because so little of its rock survives. That's why Kansas, now about as far from an ocean
as you can get, is one of the best places in the world to find Cretaceous sea reptiles.

### A greenhouse climate

The Cretaceous was warm. For most of it there were no permanent polar ice caps, and forests grew close to both poles.
Dinosaurs lived there too: duck-billed dinosaurs and small tyrannosaurs in northern Alaska, and small plant-eaters at
Dinosaur Cove in Victoria, Australia, which was then inside the Antarctic Circle. They lived through months of winter
darkness, even if they didn't face the frozen winters of today's polar regions.

The hottest stretch came near the start of the Late Cretaceous, around 94 million years ago, when ocean temperatures
peaked and large parts of the sea floor ran short of oxygen, an event geologists call Oceanic Anoxic Event 2. Things
cooled somewhat toward the end of the period, but the world stayed far warmer than ours.

## The flowering-plant revolution

If you could visit the start of the Cretaceous, the forests would look strange in one specific way: there were no
flowers. The plants were conifers, cycads, ginkgoes, ferns and horsetails, the same kinds of plants the Jurassic
sauropods had eaten.

Flowering plants (angiosperms) show up in the Early Cretaceous. Some of the earliest well-known fossils include
*Montsechia*, a water plant from Spain about 130 million years old, and *Archaefructus*, from the lake beds of
northeastern China, described in 2002. They were small and weedy, not grand trees.

Then they spread. By the Late Cretaceous, flowering plants made up a large share of many floras, including early
relatives of magnolias, sycamores, laurels and water lilies. Grasses were around too: grass fragments have been found
in fossil dung from Late Cretaceous titanosaurs in India, which means at least some sauropods were grazing.

Flowers brought pollinators with them. The oldest known bee, *Melittosphex*, is preserved in Burmese amber about 100
million years old. Butterflies and moths, beetles and wasps all diversified alongside the new plants.

For dinosaurs, this meant a changing menu. Some of the most successful Late Cretaceous plant-eaters, the duck-billed
hadrosaurs and the horned ceratopsians, had batteries of hundreds of stacked teeth for grinding tough plants. They
became the cows and antelopes of their world.

## Early Cretaceous dinosaurs

The Early Cretaceous (about 145 to 100.5 million years ago) is when the dinosaur cast starts to look familiar. Sauropods
were still around but no longer ruled every landscape, and new groups were taking over.

- **Iguanodon** was the plant-eater of the age in Europe. In 1878, coal miners at Bernissart, Belgium, found dozens of
  skeletons in a single mine, still among the most famous dinosaur finds ever.
- **Utahraptor** and **Deinonychus** were large, sickle-clawed raptors in North America. *Deinonychus*, described by
  John Ostrom in 1969 from Montana, is the animal that convinced scientists dinosaurs could be active and bird-like.
- **Acrocanthosaurus**, a tall-spined predator from Oklahoma and Texas, was one of the biggest meat-eaters of its time.
- **Baryonyx**, found by an amateur fossil hunter in a clay pit in Surrey, England, in 1983, had a long, crocodile-like
  snout and fish scales in its stomach.
- **Psittacosaurus**, a small, parrot-beaked early relative of *Triceratops*, was so common in Asia that it's known
  from hundreds of specimens.
- **Microraptor**, from the lake beds of northeastern China, was a crow-sized dinosaur with flight feathers on its arms
  *and* legs.

The same Chinese lake beds (the Jehol Biota, around 120 to 125 million years old) are where most feathered dinosaurs
have been found, preserved in fine volcanic ash along with early birds, mammals, insects and some of the first flowers.

## Late Cretaceous: the dinosaurs' last and greatest age

The **Late Cretaceous** (100.5 to 66 million years ago) gets the most attention, and fairly. It had more kinds of
dinosaurs than any earlier time, and many of the most familiar ones.

It opened with giants. Around 100 to 95 million years ago, South America had the titanosaurs *Patagotitan* and
*Argentinosaurus*, among the heaviest land animals that ever lived, hunted by *Giganotosaurus*. In North Africa,
*Spinosaurus* waded the rivers after fish. (The original *Spinosaurus* fossils, found in Egypt, were destroyed when
Munich was bombed in 1944, which is part of why the animal is still argued over.)

Later, the northern continents filled with new families:

- **Tyrannosaurs** rose from small Jurassic ancestors to become the top predators of North America and Asia.
- **Hadrosaurs**, the duck-billed dinosaurs, became the most common large plant-eaters. *Maiasaura* in Montana was
  found with nests, eggs and babies, evidence that its young were cared for. *Parasaurolophus* had a hollow crest it
  probably used to make sound.
- **Ceratopsians** grew horns and frills: *Protoceratops* in Mongolia, then *Triceratops* at the very end.
- **Ankylosaurs** like *Ankylosaurus* carried bony armor and a tail club.
- **Raptors and their relatives** spread across Asia. The famous "Fighting Dinosaurs" fossil from Mongolia, found in
  1971, shows a *Velociraptor* and a *Protoceratops* locked together, probably buried mid-fight by a collapsing dune.

By the final few million years, western North America had *T. rex*, *Triceratops*, *Edmontosaurus*, *Ankylosaurus* and
*Pachycephalosaurus* all in the same landscape. That world is preserved in the
[Hell Creek Formation](/blogs/blog/hell-creek-formation) of Montana and the Dakotas, the best record we have of the
last dinosaurs.

## Cretaceous Period animals beyond the dinosaurs

Searchers asking about **Cretaceous Period animals** usually mean dinosaurs, but some of the period's strangest
creatures weren't dinosaurs at all.

| Animal | What it was | Why it's notable |
|---|---|---|
| **Mosasaurs** | Giant marine lizards, related to monitor lizards and snakes | Top predators of Late Cretaceous seas, some 12 m or more |
| **Plesiosaurs** (e.g. *Elasmosaurus*) | Long-necked marine reptiles | *Elasmosaurus* from Kansas had a neck of more than 70 vertebrae |
| **Quetzalcoatlus** | A pterosaur | One of the largest flying animals ever, wingspan around 10 m, found in Texas |
| **Archelon** | A sea turtle | More than 4 m long, found in the Pierre Shale of South Dakota |
| **Deinosuchus** | A giant crocodylian | Big enough to prey on dinosaurs, with its bite marks found on dinosaur bones |
| **Ammonites** | Coiled-shell relatives of squid | Everywhere in Cretaceous seas; used to date the rocks |
| **Mammals** | Mostly small, shrew- to badger-sized | *Repenomamus*, a badger-sized Chinese mammal, was found with a baby *Psittacosaurus* in its stomach |
| **Birds** | Toothed sea birds and early modern birds | *Asteriornis*, from Belgium about 66.7 million years ago, is one of the oldest known modern birds, close to the ancestor of ducks and chickens |

The birds matter most to the story's ending. They're dinosaurs too, and they're the branch that made it through.

## How did the Cretaceous Period end?

About 66 million years ago, an asteroid roughly 10 km (6 miles) across hit what is now the Yucatán Peninsula in Mexico,
making the Chicxulub crater. The impact threw vaporized rock, soot and sulfur into the atmosphere, causing a sudden
darkness and global cooling that collapsed food chains on land and in the sea.

Around three-quarters of species disappeared. All the non-bird dinosaurs went, along with the pterosaurs, mosasaurs,
plesiosaurs and ammonites. Small mammals, birds, crocodylians, turtles, frogs and many plants survived, and the world
that followed, the Paleogene, belonged to them.

Volcanoes in India (the Deccan Traps) were erupting around the same time, and scientists have argued about their
role. The current weight of evidence points to the impact as the main cause. The layer that records all of this is
the subject of our full guide to [the K-Pg boundary](/blogs/blog/k-pg-boundary).

## FAQ

**When was the Cretaceous Period?**
From about 145 million years ago to 66 million years ago. It's the third and last period of the Mesozoic Era, after
the Triassic and Jurassic.

**How long did the Cretaceous Period last?**
About 79 million years, the longest geologic period since the start of the Cambrian, about 540 million years ago.

**What does "Cretaceous" mean?**
Chalky. It comes from the Latin *creta*, chalk, after the thick chalk beds of Europe that formed in Cretaceous seas.

**Why is the Cretaceous abbreviated K?**
From *Kreide*, German for chalk. It avoids confusion with the Cambrian and Carboniferous, which use C.

**What was the climate like in the Cretaceous?**
Much warmer than today. For most of the period there were no permanent ice caps, sea levels were very high, and
forests grew near the poles.

**What dinosaurs lived in the Cretaceous Period?**
Most of the famous ones: *Tyrannosaurus rex*, *Triceratops*, *Velociraptor*, *Spinosaurus*, *Ankylosaurus*,
*Iguanodon*, *Parasaurolophus*, *Giganotosaurus* and *Argentinosaurus*, among hundreds of others.

**Did T. rex live in the Jurassic or the Cretaceous?**
The Cretaceous, right at the end, about 68 to 66 million years ago. *Stegosaurus* and *Allosaurus* are Jurassic.

**What is the difference between the Early and Late Cretaceous?**
The Early Cretaceous ran from about 145 to 100.5 million years ago and the Late Cretaceous from 100.5 to 66 million.
The Early Cretaceous saw the first flowering plants and animals like *Iguanodon* and *Deinonychus*; the Late Cretaceous
had the duck-bills, horned dinosaurs, tyrannosaurs and the giant titanosaurs.

**What ended the Cretaceous Period?**
The Chicxulub asteroid impact 66 million years ago, which caused a mass extinction that killed all non-bird dinosaurs.
