# Dinosaur Scavenger Hunt: 5 Ready-to-Use Hunts With Clues for Ages 2–12

You have a dinosaur-mad kid, a rainy Saturday (or a birthday party in three weeks), and about
twenty minutes to set something up. A dinosaur scavenger hunt is the answer: it gets kids moving,
it works for one child or fifteen, and it costs nothing but paper. Below are five complete hunts,
sorted by age, with every clue written out and every hiding spot named, so you can copy them onto
index cards and go.

Start with the table, pick your hunt, and skip straight to it.

## Which dinosaur scavenger hunt should you run?

| Hunt | Ages | Reading needed? | Where | Setup time | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Picture hunt | 2–4 | No | Indoors | 10 min | Toddlers, one or two kids |
| 2. Rhyming clue trail | 5–8 | A little (or a grown-up reads) | Indoors | 15 min | A rainy afternoon |
| 3. Backyard fossil hunt | 4–10 | No | Outdoors | 5 min | Park days, camping |
| 4. Party puzzle hunt | 4–9 | No | Either | 20 min | Birthday parties, 5–15 kids |
| 5. Fact-code hunt | 8–12 | Yes | Indoors | 25 min | Older kids who think it's "too babyish" |

Each one ends with a "fossil" at the finish: a prize box, a small treat, or a puzzle they assemble
together. More on treasure at the end.

## Hunt 1: The picture hunt (ages 2–4)

Toddlers can't read clues, and they don't want to wait their turn. So skip the clues entirely.
Hide eight small toy dinosaurs around one room, and give each child a card with a simple drawing
of each one (or tape a photo of each toy to the card). When they find a dinosaur, they bring it
back and you tick its picture.

**Hiding spots that work at this age:**

1. Peeking out of a shoe by the door
2. On the bottom shelf of a bookcase, between two books
3. Under a couch cushion, head sticking out
4. In the bathtub (dry)
5. Inside a mixing bowl on a low kitchen chair
6. On a windowsill, "looking outside"
7. In the toy box, on top of everything else
8. Under a blanket on the bed, tail showing

**The rule that makes it work:** leave part of every dinosaur visible. A two-year-old who searches
for a minute and finds nothing gives up, and you lose the game. A tail poking out from a cushion
is exactly hard enough.

**Make it last longer:** once all eight are found, the kids hide them and you search. Three-year-olds
reliably hide every dinosaur in the same spot, and finding it with a big gasp never stops being funny
to them.

## Hunt 2: The rhyming clue trail (ages 5–8)

This is the classic clue-to-clue hunt. Each card sends the kids to the next hiding place, and the
last card leads to the treasure. Every clue is built on a true dinosaur fact, so they learn something
while they run.

Write each clue on an index card. Hide **Clue 2** at the spot Clue 1 describes, **Clue 3** at the spot
Clue 2 describes, and so on. You hand them Clue 1 to start.

| Card | The clue | Hide the next card… |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *"T. rex had teeth as long as bananas, it's true. Go find where the fruit waits for you."* | In the fruit bowl |
| 2 | *"Dinosaurs laid eggs, just like a hen. Look where the eggs stay cold, and then…"* | In the fridge, by the eggs |
| 3 | *"Stegosaurus had a brain about the size of a lime. Go where you sit down to read at bedtime."* | Under the pillow or on the reading chair |
| 4 | *"Some dinosaurs had feathers, soft and light. Look where your coat hangs, out of sight."* | In a coat pocket or on the coat hook |
| 5 | *"Brachiosaurus stretched up high to eat. Look somewhere tall, but not at your feet."* | On top of the fridge or a high shelf (grown-up retrieves) |
| 6 | *"Fossils are found buried down deep. Look in the place where the shoes go to sleep."* | Inside a shoe or in the shoe rack |
| 7 | *"Dinosaurs drank from rivers and lakes. Go where the water runs, for goodness' sakes."* | By the kitchen sink |
| 8 | *"Triceratops had three horns on its face. Count to three, then check where you wash your face."* | On the bathroom mirror |
| 9 | *"Ankylosaurus had a club on its tail. Go knock on the door where we get the mail."* | On the front door handle |
| 10 | *"You followed the tracks like a paleontologist would. Your fossil is hiding where the snacks smell good."* | The treasure, in the pantry or snack cupboard |

**Tips from running this one:**

- **Number the cards** in a corner. If a card goes missing, you'll know which one, and you can
  point them to the next spot.
- **With non-readers in the group,** let a grown-up or older sibling read every card aloud, and let
  the youngest kid be the one who grabs the next card.
- **With more than one child,** hand the cards out in turns ("Maya reads this one, Leo reads the next").
  Otherwise the fastest reader runs the whole hunt and everyone else trails behind.
- **Ten clues takes 15–20 minutes** for most six-year-olds. For a five-year-old, cut it to six.

If they love the clue cards, the trail works with riddles too. Swap in the three-clue "What am I?"
cards from our [dinosaur riddles](/blogs/blog/dinosaur-riddles) post and hide the answer dinosaur
at each spot.

## Hunt 3: The backyard fossil hunt (ages 4–10)

Real paleontologists spend most of their time walking slowly and looking at the ground. This hunt is
that, for a backyard, a park or a camping trip. Nothing to hide. Hand each kid a list (draw pictures
for the little ones) and a paper bag, and send them out.

**The field paleontologist's checklist:**

- [ ] A rock with a stripe or a line in it (that's a layer, the way fossils get buried over time)
- [ ] A rock that's smooth all over (water wore it down, the same way rivers buried dinosaur bones)
- [ ] A leaf bigger than your hand (plant-eaters like Brachiosaurus needed huge amounts of leaves)
- [ ] A fern or something that looks like one (ferns were around long before the dinosaurs, and still are)
- [ ] A pinecone (conifers fed dinosaurs too)
- [ ] A feather (birds are living dinosaurs, so this one counts as a real dinosaur find)
- [ ] A footprint in mud, sand or snow, any animal's (dinosaur tracks are fossils too, called trace fossils)
- [ ] A stick shaped like a bone
- [ ] Something that's three different colors
- [ ] A bird you can see or hear (point at it: that's a dinosaur)

**The finish:** spread every find on a towel and "label the dig." Kids pick their best fossil and
explain it to everyone. Older kids can sort the haul into "rock," "plant" and "animal," which is the
same first sort a dig site does.

**Bring a bag for the feather and wash hands after.** Wild bird feathers are fine to look at but not
for putting in mouths.

## Hunt 4: The party puzzle hunt (ages 4–9)

This is the one for a dinosaur birthday party. Instead of a single prize at the end, the hunt *is* the
prize: kids find the pieces of a jigsaw puzzle hidden around the house or yard, then build it together.
It solves the biggest party-hunt problem, which is one kid finding the treasure and nine kids crying.

**How to set it up:**

1. Use a puzzle with big pieces. A 30-piece puzzle works for up to ten kids (three pieces each).
2. Put 3 pieces into each of ten small envelopes or plastic eggs. Write a number on each.
3. Hide the envelopes. For ages 4–5, in plain sight at a kid's eye level. For 6–9, behind cushions,
   under plates, inside a cup.
4. Each kid finds **one** envelope and then helps the others (assign numbers if you have a group that
   grabs).
5. Everyone meets at a table and builds the picture. The birthday kid places the last piece.

## Hunt 5: The fact-code hunt (ages 8–12)

Older kids get bored of rhymes fast. Give them a hunt where they have to *know* something (or look it
up) before they can find the next clue. Each card asks a dinosaur question. The answer is a number, and
the number tells them where to go.

**Set up a number key first.** Tape this to the fridge or hand out a copy:

| Number | Go to… |
|---|---|
| 1 | The front door (a decoy) |
| 2 | Under the kitchen table |
| 3 | The bookshelf |
| 12 | The bathroom |
| 40 | The laundry basket |
| 66 | Behind the TV |
| 150 | The garage or the back step |

**The clues** (hide each one at the spot the previous answer sends them to):

| Card | Question | Answer → go to |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | How many horns did Triceratops have on its face? | 3 → bookshelf |
| 2 | How many working fingers did T. rex have on each hand? | 2 → kitchen table |
| 3 | About how many feet long was Sue, the famous T. rex skeleton in Chicago? (Round to the nearest ten: 30 or 40?) | 40 → laundry basket |
| 4 | How many million years ago did the non-bird dinosaurs die out? | 66 → behind the TV |
| 5 | About how many million years ago did Stegosaurus live? (Pick: 66 or 150) | 150 → back step |
| 6 | How many hearts would you need for a whole herd of 12 Brachiosaurus? | 12 → bathroom, where the treasure waits |

The front-door number is a decoy: nothing in the questions gives 1. A kid who guesses instead of
working it out ends up standing at an empty door, which teaches the point of the game fast.

**Make it harder:** don't give the number key at all. Hide the key itself at the start, and make the
first clue "Find the key: it's somewhere a paleontologist keeps their notes" (your desk or a notebook).

## How to set up any dinosaur scavenger hunt in 15 minutes

Whichever hunt you pick, the same few things make it go well:

1. **Walk the route yourself** after hiding everything. You'll find the card that slid behind the
   radiator before a six-year-old does.
2. **Take a phone photo of each hiding spot.** If a clue goes missing, you can show them.
3. **One color per kid** for big groups. Each child hunts only for envelopes, eggs or cards in their
   color. Everyone finds the same amount and nobody hoards.
4. **Put the treasure somewhere a grown-up can see.** The end is the part you want to watch.
5. **Keep it short.** Ten minutes for toddlers, fifteen to twenty for ages 5–8, up to half an hour
   for older kids. Stop while they still want more.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many clues should a dinosaur scavenger hunt have?**
Six for ages 4–5, eight to ten for ages 6–8, and up to twelve for older kids. Each clue takes a
child about one to two minutes, so ten clues runs fifteen to twenty minutes.

**How do you do a scavenger hunt for kids who can't read?**
Use pictures instead of words. Draw or print a picture of each hiding spot (a shoe, the fridge,
the bathtub) or of each thing to find. Hunt 1 above is built for this.

**What's the difference between a scavenger hunt and a treasure hunt?**
In a scavenger hunt, kids collect a list of things. In a treasure hunt, clues lead them from spot
to spot to one prize at the end. Hunts 1 and 3 are scavenger hunts, Hunts 2 and 5 are treasure
hunts, and Hunt 4 is a bit of both.

**How do you stop one kid from finding everything at a party?**
Give each child a color, or a number, and have them find only their own envelope. Then they all
help each other. The puzzle hunt (Hunt 4) also fixes it, because nobody can finish without
everybody's pieces.

**Can you do a dinosaur scavenger hunt outside in winter?**
Yes. Snow is great for the tracks item on the fossil checklist, and you can hide dinosaurs in snowbanks
with their tails out. Cut the list to five items so hands don't get too cold.

**Are birds really dinosaurs?**
Yes. Birds evolved from small feathered dinosaurs and scientists class them as living dinosaurs.
That's why a feather and a bird both count on the fossil hunt checklist.
