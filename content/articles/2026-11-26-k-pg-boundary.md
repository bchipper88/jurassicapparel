# The K-Pg Boundary: The Thin Layer of Clay Where the Dinosaurs End

There's a place in a gorge outside Gubbio, Italy, where you can lay a finger across 66 million years. Below it,
limestone full of the shells of Cretaceous plankton. Above it, limestone with almost none of them. In between is a
band of reddish clay about a centimeter thick. That band is the **K-Pg boundary**, and it's the closest thing the
Earth has to a recording of the day an asteroid hit.

It turns up all over the world: in sea-cliffs in Denmark, roadcuts in Colorado, badlands in Montana, drill cores off
Antarctica. Wherever rocks of the right age survive, the line is there, and it carries the same fingerprints of the
same impact.

This guide covers what the boundary is, why it's called "K-Pg" (and why your textbook may say "K-T"), the evidence
packed into that thin layer, how a father-and-son team cracked it, and where you can go and see it for yourself.

## The K-Pg boundary at a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | The rock layer that marks the end of the Cretaceous Period and the start of the Paleogene |
| **When** | About 66.0 million years ago (argon-argon dates put it within a few tens of thousands of years of 66.04 million) |
| **What happened** | A roughly 10 km (6 mile) asteroid hit the Yucatán Peninsula, Mexico, making the Chicxulub crater |
| **What it looks like** | Usually a thin clay layer, from a few millimeters to a few centimeters thick, far from the crater; meters of jumbled debris close to it |
| **Key evidence** | Iridium, shocked quartz, glassy spherules, soot, tsunami deposits, an abrupt change in fossils |
| **What died** | Around three-quarters of species, including all non-bird dinosaurs, pterosaurs, mosasaurs, plesiosaurs and ammonites |
| **Official reference point** | A section near El Kef, Tunisia, chosen by geologists as the global standard |
| **Old name** | The K-T (Cretaceous–Tertiary) boundary |

## Why "K-Pg"? And what happened to "K-T"?

The two letters stand for the two periods on either side of the line.

**K** is the Cretaceous. It isn't a C because "Cretaceous" comes from the Latin *creta*, chalk, and geologists
borrowed the abbreviation from the German word for chalk, *Kreide*. It also keeps the Cretaceous from being confused
with the Cambrian and the Carboniferous, which already have dibs on C.

**Pg** is the Paleogene, the period that starts right after it and runs to about 23 million years ago.

For most of the 20th century the same line was called the **K-T boundary**, the T standing for "Tertiary." The
Tertiary was an old, informal name for the whole stretch of time from the dinosaurs' end to the Ice Ages. The
International Commission on Stratigraphy dropped it from the official timescale in the 2000s and split the time into
the Paleogene and the Neogene, so the line became K-Pg.

Same layer, same event, same 66 million years. If a book, a museum sign or your kid's homework says K-T, it means
exactly this. You'll also see it written K/Pg, KPg or "the Cretaceous-Paleogene boundary."

## What's actually in the boundary layer

The boundary isn't one thing. It's a stack of evidence, and each piece rules out a different alternative explanation.

### Iridium

**Iridium** is a heavy metal that's extremely rare in the Earth's crust, because most of the planet's supply sank
into the core while the Earth was molten. Meteorites and asteroids have far more of it. So when a layer of ordinary
clay contains many times the normal amount of iridium, something from space probably delivered it.

At Gubbio, the boundary clay holds about 30 times the iridium of the rocks around it. At Stevns Klint in Denmark the
figure is higher still. That spike, now found at more than 100 sites worldwide, is the signature everyone looks for
first.

### Shocked quartz

Quartz is tough. Squeeze it hard enough, fast enough, and it develops sets of microscopic parallel planes, like
scratches running through each grain. Only two things on Earth make that pressure: impacts and nuclear explosions.
In 1984, Bruce Bohor and colleagues found these **shocked quartz** grains in the boundary clay in eastern Montana,
which was a big blow to the idea that volcanoes alone could explain the iridium.

### Spherules and glass

Molten rock thrown out of the crater cooled in flight into tiny beads, called **spherules**, from sand-grain size to
a few millimeters. Close to the crater, in Haiti and Mexico, some are still glass. Farther away they've mostly
weathered into clay, but their round shapes survive. Many contain nickel-rich spinel crystals, another marker of an
impact.

### Soot

In 1985, Wendy Wolbach and colleagues found the boundary clay is loaded with **soot**, enough to suggest widespread
fires or burning of hydrocarbon-rich rocks at the impact site. Exactly how much of the world burned is still
argued over, but the soot is there.

### Tsunami and debris deposits

Around the Gulf of Mexico the boundary looks completely different. Instead of a neat clay seam, there are meters of
churned-up sand, pebbles and broken shells, dumped by giant waves and by the shaking ground. Along the Brazos River in
Texas, geologists have studied these tsunami beds for decades.

### The fossils change

This is the part that matters most to dinosaur fans. Below the line, Cretaceous fossils. Above it, a different world:

- **In the ocean,** most species of tiny shelled plankton (foraminifera and coccolithophores) vanish at the line, and
  the ammonites are gone for good.
- **On land,** pollen records show many flowering plants disappearing at once.
- **Right above the boundary,** in western North America, comes the **fern spike**: a layer where fern spores
  suddenly dominate the pollen record. Ferns are what grow back first on bare, burned or volcanic ground today. The
  spike, described by Robert Tschudy and colleagues in 1984, looks like the aftermath of a landscape wiped clean.
- **No non-bird dinosaur bones** turn up in place above the boundary anywhere in the world.

### Two layers in the American West

In Colorado, New Mexico, Montana and North Dakota, the boundary often splits into two thin bands. The lower one, a
couple of centimeters thick, is mostly ejecta: spherules and debris that rained out of the sky within minutes to
hours. The upper one, just a few millimeters, holds the iridium and the shocked quartz, the finest dust that settled
out over the following months. Two layers, one event, sorted by how long each part stayed aloft.

## How the K-Pg boundary was cracked

In the 1970s, geologist **Walter Alvarez** was studying the rocks at Gubbio. He wanted to know how long it took that
centimeter of clay to form: a few years, or thousands? He enlisted his father, **Luis Alvarez**, a Nobel
Prize-winning physicist, with nuclear chemists **Frank Asaro** and **Helen Michel**.

Their idea was to measure iridium, which arrives in a slow, steady drizzle of space dust. A lot of iridium would mean
the clay took a long time to pile up. What they found was far too much, around 30 times the expected level. Either the
clay took an absurdly long time to form, or something huge from space had dumped iridium all at once.

They found the same spike in Denmark and New Zealand, and in 1980 published their conclusion in *Science*: an
asteroid about 10 km across hit the Earth and caused the extinction. That same year, Dutch geologist **Jan Smit**
independently reported an iridium anomaly at Caravaca, Spain.

The idea was controversial for a decade, mostly because there was no crater. The answer was already sitting in oil
company files. In 1978, geophysicists **Glen Penfield** and **Antonio Camargo**, working for Mexico's state oil
company, had found a huge buried ring structure under the Yucatán. In 1991, **Alan Hildebrand** and colleagues
connected it to the boundary and named it the **Chicxulub crater**, after a town near its center.

In 2010, 41 scientists published a joint review in *Science* concluding the impact was the cause of the mass
extinction. In 2016 a drilling expedition cored into the crater's peak ring, the mountains that rebounded in its
center, and brought up rock shattered and melted by the impact.

## Chicxulub: the crater behind the line

The crater is buried under up to a kilometer of younger rock, so you can't see it from the ground. What gives it away
on the surface is a ring of sinkholes (cenotes) on the Yucatán that traces its rim, and gravity and magnetic maps that
show its shape.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Diameter** | About 180 km (110 miles) |
| **Impactor** | Around 10 km (6 miles) across |
| **Location** | Half on land under the Yucatán Peninsula, half under the Gulf of Mexico |
| **Named for** | The town of Chicxulub Puerto, near the center |
| **Target rock** | Limestone and sulfur-rich evaporites, which matters (see below) |

Where it hit made a difference. The rock there was full of sulfur-bearing minerals, and vaporizing them likely put a
huge amount of sulfate haze into the upper atmosphere. Together with dust and soot, that would have dimmed the
sunlight, cooled the planet and shut down photosynthesis for months to years: an "impact winter." That, more than the
blast itself, is what killed most things far from the Yucatán.

## Wasn't it the volcanoes?

You may have seen the other suspect: the **Deccan Traps**, a vast pile of lava flows in western India that erupted
over hundreds of thousands of years on both sides of the boundary. Volcanoes that big change the climate, and for
decades some scientists argued they did the real damage.

The current weight of evidence points to the asteroid as the main cause. A 2020 study in *Science* by Pincelli Hull
and colleagues looked at ocean records across the boundary and found the climate shifts from the volcanism came well
before the extinction, while the collapse lined up with the impact. The Deccan eruptions may have stressed some
ecosystems in advance, and how much they mattered is still researched. But the sharp line in the rocks matches the
day of the impact, not the slow pulse of the lava.

## What made it across

The boundary is a line of death, but not a clean sweep. Here's who survived:

- **Birds.** They are dinosaurs, so dinosaurs technically made it. Mostly small, ground-living, seed-eating birds.
- **Mammals,** mostly small ones. Within a million years they were diversifying fast, as the fossils at Corral
  Bluffs, Colorado (Lyson et al. 2019) show.
- **Crocodilians, turtles, lizards, snakes, frogs and salamanders.** Freshwater animals did unusually well, perhaps
  because rivers and lakes fed on dead plant matter and were buffered from the worst of the cold.
- **Plants** came back from seeds, roots and spores, starting with those ferns.

The pattern: small, able to eat whatever was around (seeds, insects, rotting leaves), and able to shelter. Big animals
that needed a lot of fresh food every day had no chance.

## Where you can see the K-Pg boundary

You don't need a drill rig. The boundary is exposed at the surface in a number of places, and some are set up for
visitors. Check with each site before you go about access, trails and the rules on touching or collecting.

| Site | Country | What you'll see |
|---|---|---|
| **Bottaccione Gorge, Gubbio** | Italy | The section where the Alvarez team found the iridium, in pink limestone along the road |
| **Stevns Klint** | Denmark | A 15 km chalk sea-cliff with the dark "fish clay" boundary layer; a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2014 |
| **El Kef** | Tunisia | The official global reference section for the boundary |
| **Zumaia** | Spain | Tilted layers on the Basque coast with the boundary in a long cliff sequence |
| **Trinidad Lake State Park** | Colorado, USA | A marked trail to the boundary layer in the Raton Basin, one of the easiest places to see it in North America |
| **Raton Pass area** | New Mexico / Colorado, USA | Roadside exposures of the two-layer boundary in coal-bearing rocks |
| **Hell Creek area** | Montana / North Dakota, USA | The boundary clay above the last dinosaur bones; see our [Hell Creek Formation field guide](/blogs/blog/hell-creek-formation) |
| **Brazos River** | Texas, USA | Tsunami deposits from the impact, closer to the crater |

At most of these, the boundary looks unimpressive: a darker or redder seam, sometimes only as thick as a pencil. That's
the point. The biggest event in the last 66 million years left a line you could miss if no one pointed at it.

## Why the boundary matters for dinosaur fans

The K-Pg boundary is the reason paleontologists can say with confidence when the last *T. rex* and *Triceratops*
lived. In the badlands of Montana and the Dakotas, their bones sit in river sands beneath the line and never above it.
Our guide to [where T. rex lived](/blogs/blog/where-did-t-rex-live) walks through the places its fossils come from,
nearly all of them just below this layer.

It's also why the dinosaurs we know best are some of the very last ones. The rocks just under the boundary in western
North America are some of the most searched in the world, precisely because people wanted to know what was alive at
the end.

## K-Pg boundary facts for kids

1. The K-Pg boundary is a thin layer of rock that marks the day an asteroid hit Earth, about 66 million years ago.
2. In most places it's only about as thick as your finger, or thinner.
3. The asteroid was about 6 miles wide, about the size of a city.
4. It made a crater about 110 miles across, now buried under Mexico.
5. The clay has lots of iridium, a metal that's rare on Earth but common in space rocks.
6. Scientists also find tiny glass beads in it: drops of melted rock that flew through the air.
7. "K" is for Cretaceous, from the German word for chalk.
8. No T. rex or Triceratops bones have ever been found above the line.
9. Birds are dinosaurs that survived, so there are dinosaurs at your bird feeder.
10. Right after the boundary, ferns took over, like weeds on an empty lot.

## FAQ

**What is the K-Pg boundary?**
The layer in the rock record that marks the end of the Cretaceous Period and the start of the Paleogene, about 66
million years ago. In most places it's a thin clay layer containing debris from the Chicxulub asteroid impact, and it
coincides with the extinction of the non-bird dinosaurs.

**Is the K-Pg boundary the same as the K-T boundary?**
Yes. K-T (Cretaceous–Tertiary) is the older name. "Tertiary" was dropped from the official geologic timescale, so the
boundary is now called K-Pg, for Cretaceous–Paleogene. It's the same layer and the same event.

**How old is the K-Pg boundary?**
About 66.0 million years old. Precise argon-argon dating published in 2013 put it at about 66.04 million years ago,
with an uncertainty of a few tens of thousands of years.

**How thick is the K-Pg boundary layer?**
Far from the crater, usually a few millimeters to a few centimeters, about a centimeter at Gubbio, Italy. Near the
Gulf of Mexico, impact debris and tsunami deposits can be meters thick.

**Why does the K-Pg boundary have iridium?**
Iridium is rare in the Earth's crust but much more common in asteroids. The asteroid vaporized on impact and its
iridium spread around the globe in dust, which settled into the boundary clay.

**Where is the K-Pg boundary in the United States?**
It's exposed in the Raton Basin of Colorado and New Mexico (including a marked trail at Trinidad Lake State Park), in
the Hell Creek area of Montana and North Dakota, and along the Brazos River in Texas, among other places.

**Did the asteroid kill all the dinosaurs?**
All the non-bird dinosaurs, yes. Birds descend from dinosaurs and survived, which is why scientists say dinosaurs are
still with us.

**Why is "Cretaceous" abbreviated K?**
From *Kreide*, German for chalk (the Cretaceous is named for the chalk beds of Europe). It also avoids confusion with
the Cambrian and Carboniferous periods, which use C.
