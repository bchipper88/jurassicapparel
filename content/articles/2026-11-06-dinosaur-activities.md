# Dinosaur Activities for Kids: 24 Ideas by Age, Plus a Ready-Made Dinosaur Week Plan

You have a dinosaur-obsessed kid, or a class of them, and you need something to do this afternoon that isn't another
dinosaur cartoon. These dinosaur activities are sorted by age, and most need nothing you don't already have: tape,
string, paper, a tub of sand or flour, and some toy dinosaurs. The first one takes about thirty seconds to set up.

**Start here: the Dinosaur Stomp.** Put on any music. While it plays, everyone stomps around the room like a
T. rex. When it stops, call out a dinosaur and everyone freezes in its pose: arms tucked for T. rex, on all fours with a
"spiky back" for Stegosaurus, arms out wide for a Pteranodon. Last one to freeze becomes the caller. It works for ages 2
to 7, it burns energy, and it quietly teaches dinosaur names.

At the end there's a table of everything, a five-day dinosaur week plan, and answers to the questions parents and
teachers ask most.

## Dinosaur activities for toddlers (ages 2–3)

Toddlers want to move, dump, fill and repeat. Keep each activity under ten minutes, and expect to do it four times.

### 1. Dinosaur Stomp freeze game

Described above. For two-year-olds, skip the poses: stomp while the music plays, freeze when it stops. That's
plenty.

### 2. Dinosaur footprint path

Cut 10–15 big three-toed footprints from paper (a rough "W" with a heel works) and tape them on the floor in a winding
path. Toddlers walk, hop or stomp from print to print. Change the path the next day: across the hallway, around the
coffee table, up to the bath.

### 3. Rescue the dinosaurs from tape

Lay toy dinosaurs on a baking tray and stick strips of painter's tape across them. Your toddler peels the tape off to
"rescue" each one. It's great for finger strength, and it holds attention better than you'd expect. Use painter's tape,
not packing tape, on anything you care about.

### 4. Dinosaur bath

Fill a tub or sink with warm, soapy water and give them toy dinosaurs, a sponge and an old toothbrush to scrub off the
"swamp mud." Stay within arm's reach the whole time with any water play at this age.

### 5. Hatch an egg

Hide one small dinosaur inside a plastic Easter egg, a sock, or a ball of play dough. Your toddler finds it and announces
the hatch. For a bigger version with ice eggs and a filler bin, our [dinosaur sensory bin guide](/blogs/blog/dinosaur-sensory-bin)
has the full setup, plus which fillers are safe for under-3s.

## Dinosaur activities for preschool (ages 3–5)

Preschoolers can follow two-step rules, take turns and start to count, sort and name. This is the age most dinosaur
themes are written for, and the age that gets the most out of them.

### 6. "What time is it, Mr. T. rex?"

A dinosaur version of the classic playground game. One child is the T. rex, standing with their back to the group at the
far end of the room or yard. Everyone calls "What time is it, Mr. T. rex?" The T. rex answers with a time ("three
o'clock!") and the group takes that many steps forward. When the T. rex shouts "Dinner time!" they turn and chase, and
anyone tagged becomes the next T. rex.

### 7. Dinosaur egg hunt

Fill plastic eggs with small dinosaurs, stickers or dinosaur fact cards and hide them around the room. Give each child a
basket or egg carton. For a math twist, number the eggs and have kids line them up in order once they're found. A
sheet of [dinosaur stickers](/collections/dinosaur-stickers) split across a dozen eggs is enough for a small group.

### 8. Big or small sort

Collect every toy dinosaur in the house and ask your preschooler to sort them into "big in real life" and "small in real
life." This is harder than it sounds, because toys aren't to scale. A toy Velociraptor is often the same size as a toy
Brachiosaurus, but the real Velociraptor was about the size of a turkey. Our
[Dinosaur Size Chart printable](/products/dinosaur-size-chart-kids-dinosaur-poster-printable) lines up a child next to a
Velociraptor, Stegosaurus, Triceratops, T. rex and Brachiosaurus at approximate relative scale, which settles the
argument.

### 9. Count the spikes

Draw a big Stegosaurus outline (or print one) and give your child a pile of paper triangles, buttons or blocks. Roll a
die, and add that many plates to the dinosaur's back. First to fill their back wins. It's counting practice that
doesn't feel like it.

### 10. Dinosaur alphabet hunt

Tape letter cards around the room. Call a letter, and the kids stomp to it like a dinosaur. Older preschoolers can
name a dinosaur that starts with it: A for Ankylosaurus, B for Brachiosaurus, T for Triceratops. The
[ABC Dinos alphabet printable](/products/abc-dinos-dinosaur-alphabet-printable) has all 26 capitals with a baby dinosaur
on each one, so it doubles as the wall chart.

### 11. Dinosaur yoga

Five poses, held for a slow count of five each:

- **Brachiosaurus:** stand tall on tiptoes, arms stretched straight up like a long neck.
- **Stegosaurus:** on hands and knees, round your back up high like plates.
- **Pteranodon:** stand on one foot, arms out wide like wings.
- **T. rex:** wide stance, knees bent, elbows tucked in, big roar.
- **Sleeping dinosaur egg:** curl up in a ball on the floor. Hold this one longest. It's the cool-down.

### 12. Fossil dig

Bury toy dinosaurs or dinosaur-shaped cookie cutters in a tub of sand or rice, and hand out paintbrushes and spoons.
Kids brush away the "rock" carefully, the way paleontologists do. The [sensory bin guide](/blogs/blog/dinosaur-sensory-bin)
covers this in more depth, including a cloud-dough version for kids who still put things in their mouths.

## Dinosaur activities for kindergarten to 2nd grade (ages 5–8)

Kids this age like real facts, real numbers and doing it themselves. Give them measuring tools and they'll go far.

### 13. How long was a T. rex? Measure it

Take a tape measure and a ball of string outside, to a hallway or the school gym. An adult T. rex was about 40 feet (12
meters) long from snout to tail. Measure out 40 feet of string and lay it on the ground, then have kids lie down
head-to-toe along it to find out how many of them it takes to make one T. rex. Add more lengths to compare:

| Dinosaur | Approximate length |
|---|---|
| Velociraptor | about 6 feet (2 m) |
| Triceratops | about 30 feet (9 m) |
| Stegosaurus | about 30 feet (9 m) |
| T. rex | about 40 feet (12 m) |
| Brachiosaurus | about 75–85 feet (23–26 m) |

These are typical estimates for adult skeletons. Scientists revise them as new fossils turn up, which is a good thing to
tell kids. It's a real science question, not a settled answer.

### 14. Footprint stride test

Paleontologists can estimate how fast a dinosaur was moving from the gap between its footprints. Kids can try the same
thing. Lay a long sheet of paper (or use chalk on the sidewalk), get bare feet damp, and have each child walk across it,
then run across it. Measure the gap between prints. The running strides are longer. That's the same clue scientists
read in fossil trackways.

### 15. Fossil layers in a cup

Fill a clear plastic cup with layers: sand, then soil, then crushed crackers, then more sand, pressing a small toy or
shell into one of the layers as the "fossil." Ask: which layer went in first? The bottom one. That's the rule geologists
use. In undisturbed rock, the deeper layers are older, which is how they work out roughly when a fossil was buried.

### 16. Dinosaur bowling

Set up 6–10 empty plastic bottles as "dinosaur eggs" and roll a ball at them. Write a dinosaur name on each bottle. Kids
have to say the name of every egg they knock over to keep the points. For a math version, write numbers on the bottles
and add up the score.

### 17. Draw a dinosaur from a description

Read out a description of a dinosaur without naming it: "four legs, a short tail with a club on the end, armor plates
across its back." Kids draw what they hear, then compare with the real thing (that one's Ankylosaurus). It's listening
practice and a lesson in how paleontologists reconstruct animals from partial evidence.

### 18. Dino joke and trivia relay

Split into two teams. One player from each team runs to a pile of cards at the far end, picks one, reads it aloud
(teammates can help) and runs back. Joke cards earn a point if anyone laughs; trivia cards earn a point for a right
answer. Our [dinosaur jokes](/blogs/blog/dinosaur-jokes) and [dinosaur trivia](/blogs/blog/dinosaur-trivia) pages both
have plenty, sorted by difficulty, ready to copy onto cards.

## Dinosaur activities for older kids (ages 8–12)

Older kids lose interest in anything that feels babyish. Give them scale, evidence and a little competition.

### 19. Toilet paper timeline of Earth

The single best way to show how long ago the dinosaurs lived. Let one square of toilet paper stand for 10 million years.
Earth is about 4.5 billion years old, so the whole roll-out is roughly **450 squares**: you'll need a long hallway and
probably two rolls. Starting from one end (Earth forms) and counting toward the other (today), mark:

- **Square 1:** Earth forms, about 4.5 billion years ago
- **Around square 70–100:** the first simple life, roughly 3.5–3.8 billion years ago
- **Square 427:** the first dinosaurs, about 230 million years ago (23 squares from the end)
- **Square 443:** the asteroid hits, about 66 million years ago (under 7 squares from the end)
- **Today:** the very end of the roll

Then ask where humans go. Our species is about 300,000 years old, which is roughly **three-hundredths of the last
square**. Have them tear a tiny sliver off the end. That's the moment kids actually understand deep time.

### 20. Build a dinosaur trading-card deck

Each kid picks five dinosaurs and makes a card for each one: a drawing, length, weight, diet, when it lived and a
"special move." Then they play a version of Top Trumps, comparing one stat at a time. Making the cards means looking up
real numbers, and the game means defending them.

### 21. Dinosaur debate

Give each kid or team a dinosaur and a question to argue: Who would win, Spinosaurus or T. rex? Were dinosaurs more like
reptiles or birds? (Hint: birds are living dinosaurs, which tends to start a good fight.) They get five minutes to
prepare three points, then two minutes to make their case.

### 22. Excavate a "fossil" with tools

Freeze small dinosaur figures in a block of ice, or bake them into a salt-dough "rock" (2 parts flour, 1 part salt,
roughly 1 part water, baked at a low heat until hard). Give older kids real tools with real limits: a spoon, a
paintbrush, a small wooden mallet. The challenge: get it out without breaking it, and keep a field notebook of what
they did.

### 23. Design a new dinosaur

Paleontologists describe new species all the time, and each one needs a name. Kids invent a dinosaur, then name it
properly: a Greek or Latin root for something about it (*-saurus* means lizard, *ceratops* means horned face, *raptor*
means thief or plunderer) plus the place it was found or the person who found it. Then they draw it and write the
description that would go in the scientific paper.

### 24. Dinosaur crafts that take real time

Older kids enjoy projects that stretch over a few sittings, like a cardboard skeleton model or a painted salt-dough
fossil set. Our [dinosaur crafts guide](/blogs/blog/dinosaur-crafts) has 20 craft ideas sorted by age, including the
longer builds for 7–12.

## Tips for running dinosaur activities with a group

- **Alternate loud and calm.** A stomping game followed by yoga is a plan. Two stomping games back to back is a
  problem.
- **Give every activity a real fact.** "Velociraptor was the size of a turkey" sticks better than "dinosaurs were big."
  Kids remember the surprising number.
- **Let them be the expert.** Most dinosaur kids know names you don't. Ask them. Being the one who knows is half the
  appeal.
- **Have a reward ready.** One sticker per finished activity keeps a group moving. A pack goes a long way.
- **Small figures and small kids:** anything that fits through a toilet-paper tube is a choking risk for under-3s.
  Use big figures, or skip the burying activities, for that age.

## Dinosaur activities FAQ

**What are good dinosaur activities for preschool?**
Movement games (Dinosaur Stomp, What time is it, Mr. T. rex?), a fossil dig in sand or rice, an egg hunt, sorting toy
dinosaurs by real-life size, and dinosaur yoga. Keep each one to 10–15 minutes and alternate active and calm.

**How do I do a dinosaur week at home?**
Pick one theme a day (eggs, fossils, size), and plan one active, one hands-on and one quiet activity per day. The plan
above uses only household supplies plus toy dinosaurs.

**What can I use instead of toy dinosaurs?**
Paper cut-outs, dinosaur cookie cutters, dinosaur stickers stuck on bottle caps or blocks, or printed pictures laminated
with clear tape. Most of the activities above work with any of these.

**Are dinosaur activities educational, or just fun?**
Both, if you add the fact. Measuring a T. rex is math, fossil layers are geology, the stride test is how real
paleontologists estimate speed, and naming a new dinosaur introduces Greek and Latin roots.

**What's a dinosaur activity that needs no supplies?**
Dinosaur Stomp, dinosaur yoga, What time is it, Mr. T. rex?, and the dinosaur debate. All four need only a bit of
space.
