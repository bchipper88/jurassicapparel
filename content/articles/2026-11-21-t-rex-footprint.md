# T. Rex Footprint: How Big It Was, and the Few Real Tracks We've Found

Somebody in your house wants to know whether a T. rex footprint could fit a whole kid inside it. It could, with
room to spare. A big adult *Tyrannosaurus rex* left a three-toed footprint **about 3 feet (roughly 0.9–1 m) long and
up to about 2½ feet (0.8 m) wide**. That's a print you could lie down in if you curled up a little, and about three
and a half grown-up shoes from heel to toe.

What surprises most people is how rare these prints are. T. rex skeletons number in the dozens, but footprints that
scientists are willing to pin on T. rex number only a handful. Below: how big the footprint was, the few real tracks
and where they are, how to tell what made a track, and what a line of footprints says about how fast T. rex moved.
There's also a backyard activity at the end that puts a life-size trackway on your driveway.

## T. rex footprint at a glance

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| **How long?** | About 3 ft (0.86–1 m) for a big adult |
| **How wide?** | Up to about 2½ ft (0.8 m) |
| **How many toes touched the ground?** | Three big forward-pointing toes. A small fourth (the dewclaw) sat higher on the foot and only sometimes left a mark |
| **Stride** (same foot to same foot) | About 13 ft (4 m) in the 2026 North Dakota trackway |
| **Walking speed from that trackway** | About 1.5–2 m/s, or 3.3–4.5 mph: a person's brisk walk |
| **How many known T. rex tracks?** | A handful, including two trackways (several prints in a row). The 2026 North Dakota one is the clearest |
| **Where?** | The American West: New Mexico, Montana, Wyoming and North Dakota |
| **How old?** | About 66–67 million years, near the very end of the age of dinosaurs |

## How big was a T. rex footprint?

The two best-measured examples land close together:

- **The New Mexico print** (*Tyrannosauripus pillmorei*) is about **34 inches (86 cm) long**.
- **The North Dakota prints**, described in 2026, are each **about 1 meter (3.2 ft) long and up to 0.8 m (2.6 ft)
  wide**.

To picture it:

| Compared with | How it stacks up |
|---|---|
| An adult's shoe (about 11 in / 28 cm) | Roughly 3½ shoes long |
| A 6-year-old's foot (about 7 in / 18 cm) | Around 5 kid feet long |
| A curled-up 4-year-old | Fits inside, with toes to spare |

A footprint that size is what you'd expect from the animal. As we cover in our [guide to T. rex
size](/blogs/blog/t-rex-size), a big adult stood about 12 feet tall at the hip and weighed around 8 to 9 tonnes. All
of that weight came down on two feet, one at a time.

### What the footprint looked like

A T. rex footprint is a big, broad, **three-toed** print. The middle toe is longest and points straight ahead, with
one toe angled off to each side. Behind the toes is a heavy, rounded heel pad. If you're lucky, the tips of the toes
show claw marks, and behind and to the inside you may see a small dent from the **hallux**, the short first toe that
worked like a dog's dewclaw.

That combination matters. Plenty of dinosaurs left three-toed prints. A T. rex print is distinctive because it is
**huge, wide for a meat-eater's track, and thick-toed**, and it turns up in rock of the right age in the right place.

## The handful of real T. rex tracks

Here is the short list, in the order they were found or described. Paleontologists are careful with names here, so
we've kept their wording: "probable" means probable.

### 1. Philmont Scout Ranch, New Mexico (found 1983)

Geologist **Charles Pillmore** spotted a single huge three-toed print in northern New Mexico, on land that is part of
the Philmont Scout Ranch. **Martin Lockley and Adrian Hunt** described it in 1994 and gave it the name
*Tyrannosauripus pillmorei*. The *-ipus* ending means "foot": the name, "tyrant foot," belongs to the track, not
to the animal. At about 86 cm long and about 66 million years old, it's widely
treated as the best single T. rex footprint. Its shape, its size and its age all fit, and nothing else that big and
that shape lived there at the time.

### 2. Hell Creek Formation, Montana (reported 2007)

**Manning and colleagues** reported one large tyrannosaurid track from the Hell Creek Formation of eastern
Montana. Hell Creek is the same rock that has given us many of the best T. rex skeletons. One footprint isn't a
trackway, but it showed that these prints could survive in Hell Creek's river deposits, which aren't usually kind to
footprints.

### 3. Glenrock, Wyoming (described 2016)

**Smith and colleagues** described a **probable Tyrannosaurus trackway** from the Lance Formation near Glenrock,
Wyoming. The Lance is roughly the same age as Hell Creek. "Probable" is doing honest work in that sentence. The
prints fit T. rex, but the evidence fell short of the certainty the next site gave.

### 4. Marmarth, North Dakota (found 2025, described 2026)

This is the one that made "T. rex footprint" spike in September 2026. A high school science teacher, **Kent Hups**,
found the trackway in 2025 near Marmarth, North Dakota, beside a steep clay butte. He is a co-author on the study,
with **Falkingham** and colleagues, including **Tyler Lyson**, a curator at the Denver Museum of Nature & Science. It appeared in the *Journal
of Vertebrate Paleontology* in 2026.

What they found:

- **Four footprints in a row**, making a trackway about **7 m (23 ft) long**.
- Each print about **1 m long and up to 0.8 m wide**.
- An average **stride of about 4 m (13 ft)**, though the prints are heavily weathered.
- An age of about **66.5 million years**, from the upper Hell Creek Formation.
- They're preserved as **natural casts**: sand filled the original prints and hardened, cemented by calcium
  carbonate, so the "footprints" stand up from the rock instead of sinking into it.

The authors' conclusion is worded carefully: an adult *Tyrannosaurus rex* is "the most likely trackmaker," based on
the tracks' age, location and shape. News coverage called it the first trackway of an adult T. rex. Either way, it's the best chance yet
to measure how one actually walked rather than modelling it from bones.

### And one that was wrong

In 1971 some big three-toed tracks from North America were named *Tyrannosauropus*, "tyrant lizard foot." Later work
found they were made by a **hadrosaur**, a duck-billed plant-eater, and the name was dropped. It's a good reminder
for kids and grown-ups alike: three toes and big doesn't automatically mean T. rex.

### A cousin's tracks

In British Columbia, scientists described **three parallel tyrannosaurid trackways** in 2014, now called
*Bellatoripes fredlundi*. They were made by older relatives of T. rex, not T. rex itself. Because the three walked in
the same direction, they're often cited as a hint that some tyrannosaurs moved in groups.

## Why are T. rex footprints so rare?

Footprints need a lucky sequence of events. The Natural History Museum in London explains it neatly. The ground has
to be just right, firm enough to hold a shape but soft enough to take one. Then the print has to dry and harden for
days to months, and finally get covered by mud, sand or ash before rain or a river wipes it away.

T. rex lived in a landscape of rivers and floodplains, and that's the problem. The North Dakota team suggests that
flowing water likely washed most T. rex prints away before they could be buried. Bones can survive being tumbled
downstream. A footprint can't. There's also a simple numbers game: big predators were never common, so there were
always far fewer T. rex feet hitting the mud than hadrosaur or Triceratops feet.

## How to read a dinosaur track

Once you know what to look for, any dinosaur footprint becomes a little detective puzzle. These are the clues that
ichnologists, the scientists who study tracks, actually use.

### Three ways a footprint gets preserved

| Type | What it is | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **True track** | The surface the foot actually pressed into | A dent with the sharpest detail: toe pads, sometimes claw tips |
| **Undertrack** | Layers below that got squashed by the weight | A blurrier, softer version of the print, sometimes from a few centimeters to a meter below the real surface |
| **Natural cast** | Sediment that filled the print and hardened | A raised "foot" sticking up out of the rock, like a mold turned inside out. The North Dakota prints are this kind |

Undertracks catch people out. A print that looks oddly smooth or oversized may be the ghost of a footprint made on a
surface that has since eroded away.

### Who made it? The checklist

1. **How many feet?** Prints from only the back feet mean a two-legged animal, so a theropod (meat-eater) or an
   ornithopod (such as a hadrosaur). Hand-and-foot pairs mean a four-legged animal: sauropods, ceratopsians, armored
   dinosaurs.
2. **Toe shape.** Theropod prints are longer than wide, with slender, pointed toes and a V-shaped outline. Hadrosaur
   prints are rounder, with fat, blunt toes and no sharp claw marks. That difference is what sank *Tyrannosauropus*.
3. **Size.** A 3-foot theropod print in latest-Cretaceous rock in western North America narrows things very fast,
   because T. rex was the only predator that big there.
4. **Age and place.** T. rex lived about 68 to 66 million years ago in western North America. A giant three-toed
   print in older rock, or on another continent, belongs to someone else.

### A quick theropod-or-hadrosaur test

| Clue | Theropod (meat-eater) | Hadrosaur (plant-eater) |
|---|---|---|
| Overall shape | Longer than wide | About as wide as long, or wider |
| Toes | Slender, pointed, claw tips | Thick, rounded, blunt |
| Gaps between toes | Toes spread in a V | Toes close together, like a clover leaf |
| T. rex twist | Unusually broad and thick-toed for a theropod | — |

## What T. rex footprints say about speed

Bones tell you what an animal *could* do. A trackway tells you what one individual *was doing* on one particular day.

### How scientists turn stride into speed

The classic method comes from zoologist **R. McNeill Alexander** in 1976. You need two numbers:

- **Stride length:** the distance from one footprint to the next print of the *same* foot.
- **Hip height:** estimated from the footprint itself. For big theropods, a common rule of thumb is that hip height is
  about **four times the footprint length**.

Speed comes out of a formula that combines the two. The key idea is simple: **the longer the stride compared with the
leg, the faster the animal was going.** If the stride is less than about twice the hip height, the animal was walking.
Much more than that, and it was trotting or running.

### Running the North Dakota numbers

Here's how the method plays out, as our own back-of-envelope check rather than the paper's math:

- Footprint about 1 m long, so hip height ≈ 4 × 1 m = **about 4 m**. That's close to the 12–13 ft measured on big
  skeletons.
- Stride about **4 m**, about the same as the hip height. That's comfortably under twice the hip height, so this
  T. rex was **walking**.
- Plugging those into Alexander's formula gives about **1.6 m/s**. The study's own estimate is **1.5–2 m/s, or 3.3 to
  4.5 mph**.

In other words, this T. rex was strolling at about the pace of a grown-up walking briskly to catch a bus. That's not
very dramatic, but it's real. It's the best-measured walking speed we have for an adult T. rex.

### So how fast could a T. rex run?

The footprints only show this one walk. For top speed, scientists use computer models of the skeleton and muscles.
Most recent models put an adult's top speed somewhere around **10 to 25 mph**, with the lower end more widely
accepted. Bigger animals hit a limit: beyond a certain speed, the leg bones of an 8-tonne animal would risk
breaking. Young, lighter T. rex were probably the faster sprinters. No running trackway has been found yet, so
nobody can quote a measured top speed. Anyone who gives you an exact number is giving you a model.

## Make a life-size T. rex trackway in your driveway

Every page that ranks for this search shows you a picture. This lets your kids *walk* one, and it works for one
child or a whole birthday party.

**You need:** sidewalk chalk, a tape measure, and about 25 feet of driveway, sidewalk or playground.

1. **Draw one footprint.** Make it **3 feet long and 2½ feet wide**: three thick toes fanning forward from a big
   rounded heel, with a small dewclaw mark on the inside, behind the toes.
2. **Lay out the trackway.** T. rex placed each foot about **6½ feet** (2 m) ahead of the last, alternating left and
   right, with feet only slightly apart side to side. Draw **four prints** that way. The whole line comes out at about
   23 feet, the same as the North Dakota trackway.
3. **Measure the stride.** Have a child measure from the first left print to the next left print. It should come out
   around **13 feet**. Then have them measure their own stride the same way, same foot to same foot. Most young kids
   come in at 3 to 4 feet.
4. **Walk it.** Ask them to step from print to print. They'll have to leap, which is the point. A T. rex covered that
   gap at a stroll.
5. **Stand in it.** Line kids up, toes to heel, inside one footprint and see how many fit.

Older kids can do the speed math: footprint length × 4 = hip height, then compare their own stride with their own
hip height. That's the same comparison paleontologists made in North Dakota.

## FAQ

### How big is a T. rex footprint?

About 3 feet (0.86–1 m) long and up to about 2½ feet (0.8 m) wide for a large adult. The New Mexico print is about
34 inches long, and each of the 2026 North Dakota prints is about 1 meter long.

### Where can I see a real T. rex footprint?

The New Mexico print lies within the Philmont Scout Ranch, so check with the ranch before you plan a visit. The other
sites aren't set up as public attractions as far as we know. Ask a regional museum what it has on display, since
museums sometimes show casts of tracks.

### How many T. rex footprints have been found?

Very few. There's the single New Mexico print, a single print from Montana, a probable trackway in Wyoming, and the
four-print North Dakota trackway described in 2026. That last one is the clearest, and the news coverage called it the first trackway of an adult T. rex.

### How many toes did a T. rex have?

Four toes on each foot, but only three bore its weight and showed clearly in footprints. The first toe, the hallux,
was small and set higher up, so it only sometimes left a mark.

### How fast could a T. rex run?

The North Dakota trackway shows one walking at 1.5–2 m/s (3.3–4.5 mph). Top running speed comes from computer models
of the skeleton, which mostly put adults around 10–25 mph. No running T. rex trackway has been found yet.

### How do scientists know a footprint was made by a T. rex?

They can't be completely sure, which is why the papers say "probable" or "most likely." They combine size (only T. rex
was that big among meat-eaters in its time and place), shape (three broad, thick toes with a theropod's V-shaped
layout), and the age and location of the rock.

### What's the difference between a dinosaur track and a dinosaur print?

People use them to mean the same thing. Scientists usually say **track** for a single footprint and **trackway** for
two or more footprints in a row from the same animal. A trackway is far more useful, because it gives you stride,
direction and speed.
