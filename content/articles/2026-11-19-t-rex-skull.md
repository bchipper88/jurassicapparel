# The T. Rex Skull: How Big It Was, Why It Has Holes, and the Famous Skulls You Can Still See

You're standing under a mounted *Tyrannosaurus rex* and your eye goes straight to the head. A child asks how big it
really was, why it's full of holes, and whether it's the real thing. The **T. rex skull** answers more questions about
how this animal lived than any other bone. It shows how T. rex saw, smelled and bit, and how it grew up. This guide
covers the size and weight, what each opening was for, what the skull tells us about the animal's senses, and the
famous skulls you can still go and see.

## The T. rex skull at a glance

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|

| How heavy? | The real fossil skull of Sue weighs about 600 lb (272 kg). It's stone now, not bone, so the living head was lighter. |
| Why all the holes? | They are openings (fenestrae) for jaw muscles, air spaces and the eyes. They saved weight and anchored muscle. |

| Good sense of smell? | Very. The space for its smell centres was large for a dinosaur its size. |
| Strong? | Built for one of the hardest bites of any land animal, with fused, reinforced bones over the snout. |

## How big was a T. rex skull?

The biggest T. rex skulls are about **5 feet (1.5 m) long**. That's roughly the height of an 11-year-old, and longer
than a bathtub. The Field Museum in Chicago gives Sue's skull as about 5 feet long. It's one of the largest and most
complete ever found.

Most of that length is jaw. The tooth rows run far back toward the eye. The back of the skull flares out wide, a bit
like a triangle seen from above. That width matters, and it comes up again below.

### How much does a T. rex skull weigh?

Sue's real skull is about **600 lb (272 kg)**. That's too heavy to bolt safely onto the mounted skeleton, so the
skeleton on display wears a lightweight cast. The real skull sits in its own case nearby.

Keep in mind that a fossil is rock. Over millions of years, minerals filled the spaces in the bone, so a fossil skull
weighs more than the living head's bone did. The living skull was also full of air spaces that a fossil doesn't keep.
When a skull comes out of the ground in its rock and plaster jacket, the bundle weighs a lot more. The Burke Museum's
Tufts-Love Rex skull came out of Montana in 2016 in a field jacket reported at around 2,500 lb.

## Why does a T. rex skull have so many holes?

Look at any T. rex skull and you'll see more gaps than bone. Those openings are called **fenestrae** (Latin for
"windows"), and every one had a job.

| Opening | Where | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| **Nostril (naris)** | Front tip of the snout | Air in. Leads back to the nasal passages and the smell centres. |
| **Antorbital fenestra** | Big opening in front of the eye | Held air sacs. Saved weight without weakening the snout. |
| **Orbit** | Eye socket | Held the eye. In big adults it is keyhole-shaped, with the eye in the upper part. |
| **Laterotemporal fenestra** | Behind the eye, on the side | Room for jaw muscles to bulge when they contracted. |
| **Dorsotemporal fenestra** | Top of the skull, at the back | Long read as a jaw-muscle anchor. A 2019 study argued it also held a blood-vessel network for shedding or holding heat, as in alligators. |
| **Lower-jaw opening (mandibular fenestra)** | Side of the lower jaw | Saved weight, with muscle attached around it. |

The short version is that the holes made a huge head light enough to carry. They also gave the jaw muscles a lot of
room. A solid skull 5 feet long would have been a crushing weight hanging off the front of the neck. The frame of struts
and arches carried the stress of the bite and dropped everything else.

One more opening worth knowing is the **foramen magnum**. It's the hole at the back of the skull where the spinal cord
joined the brain. It's small and easy to miss, but it's how the head connected to the body.

## What the skull tells us about how T. rex lived

### It could see forward

From above, a T. rex skull is narrow at the snout and wide at the back. That shape pointed the eyes forward, so the
two fields of view overlapped. A 2006 study by Kent Stevens estimated the overlap at about 55 degrees, more than a
modern hawk's. Overlapping vision helps judge distance, which is useful for a hunter. It also means the old "it can't
see you if you stand still" idea doesn't hold up.

### It had a very good nose

Scientists can't recover a brain from a fossil. They can, though, scan the space inside the braincase and make a
cast of it, called an endocast. In T. rex, the area for the **olfactory bulbs**, the brain's smell centres, is large.
A 2009 comparison of meat-eating dinosaurs by Darla Zelenitsky and colleagues ranked T. rex among the best smellers of
the group. A strong nose suits an animal that hunted and also scavenged. It could find a carcass from far off.

### The brain was small for the body

The braincase was a small box at the back of an enormous head. T. rex was not a genius by modern standards. Estimates
of how clever it was vary widely, and scientists still argue about them. By dinosaur standards, though, it had a large
brain for its body, and the parts for smell and balance were well developed.

### The skull was built for a crushing bite

The nasal bones along the top of the snout are **fused** into one strong ridge, and the snout is deep and broad. That
was reinforcement. T. rex bit down hard enough to crunch through bone, and fossils show it: Triceratops bones with
bite marks, and fossil droppings full of bone chips. A 2019 study by Ian Cost and colleagues found that the skull was
fairly stiff, without the loose joints some lizards have. That stiffness helped it handle the stress of those bites.
The teeth that did the biting have their own story, told in our [guide to T. rex teeth](/blogs/blog/t-rex-teeth).

### Bumps, horns and scars

Above and in front of each eye, adult T. rex skulls have roughened bony bumps. They were probably covered in keratin,
the material in fingernails, in life. Their purpose is unclear; display is the usual guess. Many skulls also show
injuries. Sue's lower jaw has several smooth-edged holes. They were long read as bite wounds, but a 2009 study
proposed a parasite infection similar to one found in modern birds. Others still read some skull scars as wounds from
fights with other tyrannosaurs.

## A young T. rex had a very different skull

A T. rex took about two decades to reach full size, and the skull changed more than any other part of the body along
the way.

- **Young animals** had long, low, narrow skulls with thinner, blade-like teeth. They looked built for speed and
  slicing, not crushing.
- **Adults** had deep, broad skulls with thick teeth and a much stronger bite.

This matters because of an argument that has run for decades. Some small tyrannosaur skulls from the same rocks were
named *Nanotyrannus*. One side holds they are a separate, smaller species, and the other that they are just young T.
rex. A 2025 study of a well-preserved young tyrannosaur skeleton argued that *Nanotyrannus* was a real species, and
the debate goes on. Either way, if you see a "baby T. rex" skull in a museum, read the label. Not everyone agrees on
what it is.

## Famous T. rex skulls you can see

| Nickname | Found | Where it is now | Why it's famous |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sue** | South Dakota, 1990 | Field Museum, Chicago | One of the largest and most complete T. rex ever found. The real skull is shown on its own, next to the mount. |
| **AMNH 5027** | Montana, 1908 | American Museum of Natural History, New York | Found by Barnum Brown's team. This skull shaped the classic T. rex look in books for a century. |
| **The Nation's T. rex** | Montana, 1988 | Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History, Washington, D.C. | A near-complete skeleton mounted in a pose of attacking a Triceratops. |
| **Stan** | South Dakota (found 1987, dug 1992) | Natural History Museum Abu Dhabi | Its skull is one of the best preserved known. Sold at auction in 2020 for $31.8 million. |
| **Tufts-Love Rex** | Montana, 2016 | Burke Museum, Seattle | A large, well-preserved skull from Hell Creek, prepared where visitors could watch. |
| **Scotty** | Saskatchewan, 1991 | Royal Saskatchewan Museum, Canada | One of the heaviest T. rex individuals by mass estimates. |

Two details people often ask about:

- **Is Sue's skull on the skeleton real?** No. The skull on the mount is a cast, because the real one is too heavy
  and too valuable to risk up there. The real skull is in a case where you can look at it closely.
- **Why did Stan sell for so much?** Fossils found on private land in the US can be sold legally, and a near-complete
  T. rex with a superb skull almost never comes up. Sue sold in 1997 for about $8.4 million, which was a record at
  the time. Stan's 2020 sale set a new one. Fossils found on US federal land belong to the public and can't be sold.

## Can you buy a T. rex skull?

Not a real one, unless you have millions to spend and the fossil came from private land. Almost everything sold as a
"T. rex skull" online is a **cast or replica**, usually resin, made from a mould of a museum specimen. Some are life
size and some are small enough for a shelf. A good listing will say which specimen it was cast from and how big it is.
If a cheap skull is described as "real fossil", be wary. At most, a product like that has a few real fossil fragments
set into a cast.

## FAQ

**How big is a T. rex skull?**
The largest are about 5 feet (1.5 m) long. Sue's skull at the Field Museum is about 5 feet long. Young animals had
much smaller, narrower skulls.

**How much does a T. rex skull weigh?**
Sue's fossil skull weighs about 600 lb (272 kg). A fossil is mineralised, so it's heavier than the living bone was. The
living head, with its air spaces, was lighter for its size.

**Why does the T. rex skull have holes in it?**
The openings, called fenestrae, made room for jaw muscles, held air sacs, and cut weight. A solid skull that size would
have been too heavy to carry well.

**Did T. rex have good eyesight?**
Its eyes pointed forward, giving it overlapping vision of about 55 degrees by one estimate. That's good for judging
distance. There's no evidence that it could only see moving things.

**Is the skull on Sue's skeleton real?**
No. The mounted skeleton wears a cast. The real skull is displayed separately at the Field Museum in Chicago.

**Can you buy a real T. rex skull?**
Real ones are extremely rare and sell for millions. Stan's skeleton sold for $31.8 million in 2020. Nearly every T. rex
skull for sale is a resin cast or replica. Fossils from US federal land can't be sold at all.

**What's the most complete T. rex skull?**
Sue and Stan are the two most often named. Stan's skull is widely described as one of the best preserved ever found.
